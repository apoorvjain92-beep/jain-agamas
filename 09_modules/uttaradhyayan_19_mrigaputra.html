Uttaradhyayan 19 · Mrigaputriya: Mrigaputra

In one line: a pampered prince sees a monk, remembers that he was once a monk himself, and answers every warning his parents give about the hard monk’s life with the far worse pains he has already suffered in hell, until they let him go to live “like a deer” and he reaches liberation.

Source: Uttaradhyayan Sutra Part 1, chapter 19 (99 verses), translated pages 441–479 (data/translations/uttaradhyayan-1/). 20 minutes.

What the chapter teaches

  1. A prince who has everything. In the lovely city of Sugriva, King Balabhadra and Queen Mriga have a son, Balashri, known as Mrigaputra (“Mriga’s son”). He is the crown prince, dear to his parents, and he lives in a palace like a garden of the gods, always enjoying himself with his wives (v.1–3).
  2. One look at a monk wakes him up. From a palace window he sees a monk walking along the road, a man of austerity, restraint and good character. Gazing at him without blinking, he thinks, “I have seen such a form before.” His mind becomes pure, delusion thins, and he remembers his past life: he came here from the world of the gods, and before that he had lived as a monk (v.4–9).
  3. He asks to leave. Turning away from pleasures, he tells his parents: “I have known the five great vows, and the suffering of hell and animal life. I am tired of this great ocean. Let me become a monk” (v.10–11).
  4. Why the world holds nothing for him. Pleasures are like poison fruit: sweet now, bitter later, and they keep suffering going. The body is impermanent and impure, a home of disease, a bubble that must burst sooner or later. Birth, old age, disease and death are all suffering. Fields, house, gold, wife, sons and this body must all be left behind (v.12–18).
  5. Dharma is food for the journey. Whoever sets out on a long road without provisions suffers hunger and thirst; whoever goes to the next life without practising dharma suffers. Whoever takes provisions travels happily. When a house is on fire the owner saves the valuables and leaves the junk; in a world burning with old age and death, “I will save my soul, if you permit me” (v.19–24).
  6. The parents: the monk’s life is too hard for you. A monk must keep thousands of virtues: equal regard for friend and enemy, no harm, no lies, not even a toothpick untaken, celibacy, owning nothing, no food at night; he must bear hunger, thirst, cold, heat, insects, abuse, beatings, begging and plucking out his hair. “You are delicate. It is like carrying a load of iron, swimming the ocean with your arms, eating a mouthful of sand, walking a sword’s edge, chewing iron grains, drinking a flame. Enjoy pleasures first, and practise dharma when you are old” (v.25–44).
  7. Mrigaputra: nothing is hard for one without craving, and I have known worse. He agrees the path is hard, but for one free of thirst for this world nothing is hard. Then he describes, in a long vivid passage, the hell he has suffered countless times: heat and cold endlessly greater than any here; being boiled in pots, burned in sand, sawn, crushed like sugarcane, torn by beings in the form of dogs and pigs, yoked to a red-hot chariot, pecked by iron-beaked birds, cut by the razor waters of the Vaitarani and the sword-leaves of the forest, fed his own roasted flesh and made to drink molten metal because he had loved meat and liquor. “Not for the blink of an eye was there any comfort there” (v.45–75). The commentary explains that the tormentors are the paramadharmika asuras, who take these forms by their powers.
  8. “Who treats the deer in the forest?” The parents give in, but ask: “As a monk you may not seek treatment when you fall ill.” He answers: when a deer falls sick in the forest, who gives it medicine, food or water? It rests under a tree, and when it recovers it goes and finds its own grass and water. “So I too will live the deer’s way (miga-chariya).” Like the deer, the monk wanders alone, lives in many places, lives on what he finds, and never scorns what he is given (v.76–84).
  9. He goes, and lives as a true monk. With his parents’ blessing (“go, son, as you wish”) he drops all attachment as a snake sheds its skin, shaking off wealth, friends, wife, son and family like dust from a cloth. He keeps the five great vows, the five carefulnesses (samiti) and three guards (gupti); free of “mine” and of ego, he is even-minded in gain and loss, pleasure and pain, life and death, praise and blame, honour and insult (v.85–94).
  10. The end, and the lesson. After practising knowledge, faith, conduct and austerity for many years, he ends his life with a month’s fast and reaches supreme liberation. The wise turn back from pleasures as the sage Mrigaputra did: knowing that wealth increases suffering and binds with fear, take up the yoke of dharma, which brings the joys of nirvana (v.95–99).

Key verses

16 · Birth is suffering

जम्मं दुक्खं जरा दुक्खं, रोगाणि मरणाणि य ।
अहो दुक्खो हु संसारो, जत्थ कीसंति जंतवो ॥१६॥

Birth is suffering, old age is suffering, and so are disease and death. Alas, the whole round of births (samsara) is suffering, where living beings are tormented.

20 · No provisions for the road

एवं धम्मं अकाऊणं, जो गच्छइ परं भवं ।
गच्छंतो सो दुही होइ, वाहीरोगेहिं पीडिओ ॥२०॥

In the same way, whoever goes on to the next life without having practised dharma suffers on the way, afflicted by sickness and disease.

45 · Nothing is hard for the one without craving

सो बिंतऽम्मा पियरो, एवमेयं जहा फुडं ।
इह लोए णिप्पिवासस्स, णत्थि किंचि वि दुक्करं ॥४५॥

He said, “Mother and father, it is just as you say, the monk’s life is hard. But for one who has no thirst for the things of this world, nothing at all is hard.”

78 · Like a deer in the forest

एगब्भूओ अरण्णे वा, जहा उ चरइ मिगो ।
एवं धम्मं चरिस्सामि, संजमेण तणेव य ॥७८॥

Just as a deer wanders alone in the forest, so I will live the dharma, by restraint and by austerity.

(As printed: तणेव. This is a misprint for तवेण, “by austerity”, as the book’s own word meanings show; the English follows the meaning.)

91 · Even-minded in all things

लाभालाभे सुहे दुक्खे, जीविए मरणे तहा ।
समो णिंदा पसंसासु, तहा माणावमाणओ ॥९१॥

He stayed the same in gain and loss, in pleasure and pain, in life and death, in blame and praise, and in honour and insult.

99 · Take up the yoke of dharma

वियाणिया दुक्खविवद्धणं धणं, ममत्तबंधं च महाभयावहं ।
सुहावहं धम्मधुरं अणुत्तरं, धारेज्ज णिव्वाण गुणावहं महं ॥९९॥

Knowing that wealth only increases suffering, and that the bond of “mine” brings great fear, take up the great, unsurpassed yoke of dharma, which brings happiness and the qualities of nirvana.
