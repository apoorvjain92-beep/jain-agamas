Acharang 2.3 · Irya (ઈર્યા): Careful Walking

In one line: a monk or nun walks only to serve restraint, stays in one place through the rains and wanders the rest of the year, watches the ground so that no living being is hurt, and meets rivers, boatmen, soldiers, hunters, wild animals and robbers with silence, care and an unshaken calm.

Source: Acharang Sutra, Book 2 (Second Shrutaskandha), chapter 3 (56 sutras, 3 sections), translated pages 208–238 (data/translations/acharang-2/). The book numbers the sutras afresh in each section, so references below read chapter.section.sutra (3.2.3 = section 2, sutra 3). 14 minutes.

What the chapter teaches

  1. Walking with care (irya samiti) has four parts. The introduction says a monk walks only for a proper purpose (serving the order, knowledge, faith and conduct), only in daylight, only on a fit path, and only with care for the six kinds of living beings. Like food and lodging, walking is needed to keep the body that carries restraint (introduction, p.208).
  2. In the rains, stay in one place; after the rains, move on. When the rains come, the paths fill with small creatures, new sprouts and webs, so the monk stops wandering and settles for the four months. He picks a place with room for study and for relieving himself, where boards and bedding and pure alms are easy to get and the place is not crowded with other beggars. When the four months and a few days more have passed and the paths are clear, he wanders again; if the paths are still full of life, he waits (3.1.1–5).
  3. Look a yoke’s length ahead and step around life. On the road the monk looks ahead about three and a half cubits. Seeing small creatures, he lengthens, shortens or turns his step, and takes another path if there is one, rather than going straight through seeds, greens, water or living earth (3.1.6–7).
  4. Avoid dangerous regions and long wildernesses. If another road is open, he does not go through border lands of robbers and people who will not listen, through kingless or warring kingdoms, or through a forest that takes many days to cross. The Kevali calls this a cause of karmic bondage: he may be taken for a spy or thief and beaten or robbed, and rain on a long road breeds life he would harm (3.1.8–10).
  5. How to cross by boat. He does not board a boat bought, borrowed, launched or bailed out for his sake. He boards a boat that crosses the river, after binding his things together, wiping his body and taking a conditional fast to death (sagari santhara). In the boat he does no work for the boatman, stays silent when told to row, bail or plug a hole, and if the boat leaks he does not raise an alarm but gives up attachment to body and goods and stays in calm absorption (samadhi) (3.1.11–19).
  6. Thrown into the river, he stays calm. If people call him dead weight and plan to throw him over, he first says he will step into the water himself. If they throw him anyway, he feels neither pleased nor upset and does not rise to hurt them. In the water he does not thrash his limbs or dive and surface; he floats, drops his things if he tires, and on the bank waits until his body dries by itself (3.2.1–8).
  7. No talk on the road; wading and rough ground. He does not walk while chatting. He wades only shallow water, below the knee, without touching limb to limb or bathing for comfort. He does not wipe muddy feet on green plants, avoids ditches, walls and pits where he might fall and clutch at plants, and goes round army camps, where a soldier may drag him as a spy, which he bears calmly (3.2.9–18).
  8. Do not stare, and walk humbly with seniors. He does not point, bend and gaze at forts, palaces, shrines, lakes or forests, for animals and birds take fright. Walking with the Acharya, Upadhyaya or a senior monk, he does not jostle them and does not speak while they answer travellers’ questions (3.3.1–6).
  9. Do not tell where the animals went. Asked by travellers about animals, plants, water, fire, grain or an army, or how big and how far a village is, he does not answer. The commentary explains that the questioner may be a hunter, and the monk would share in the killing; he stays silent, or says “I do not know” (3.2.19, 3.3.7–11).
  10. Fearless before beasts and robbers. Seeing a fierce bull or a leopard, he does not leave the road, climb a tree or plunge into water, and seeks no shelter, army or weapon. If robbers demand his cloth or bowl, he lays it on the ground, does not beg pitifully for it back, and afterwards complains to no villager or king. The commentary says the monk is himself fearless and gives fearlessness to all beings (3.3.12–16).

Key passages

3.1.1 · Stay put when the rains come

से भिक्खु वा भिक्खुणी वा से जं पुण जाणेज्जा- अब्भुवगए खलु वासावासे अभिपवुट्ठे, बहवे पाणा अभिसंभूया, बहवे बीया अहुणुब्भिण्णा, अंतरा से मग्गा बहुपाणा बहुबीया जाव संताणगा, अणभिकंता पंथा, णो विण्णाया मग्गा, सेवं णच्चा णो गामाणुगामं दूइज्जेज्जा, तओ संजयामेव वासावासं उवल्लिएज्जा ।

When a monk or nun sees that the rainy season has come and rain has fallen, that many creatures have been born and many seeds have just sprouted, that the roads are full of living beings, seeds and webs, untrodden and hard to make out, then he or she should not wander from village to village, but should settle carefully for the rainy-season stay.

3.1.6 · Look a yoke’s length ahead

से भिक्खू वा भिक्खुणी वा गामाणुगामं दुइज्जमाणे पुरओ जुगमायं पेहमाणे, दट्ठूण तसे पाणे, उद्धट्टु पायं रीएज्जा, साहट्टु पायं रीएज्जा, उक्खिप्प पायं रीएज्जा, तिरिच्छं वा कट्टु पायं रीएज्जा, सइ परक्कमे संजयामेव परक्कमेज्जा, णो उज्जुयं गच्छेज्जा, तओ संजयामेव गामाणुगामं दुइज्जेज्जा ।

Wandering from village to village, the monk or nun looks ahead a yoke’s length. Seeing small moving creatures, he walks with a long step, a short step, a raised foot or a sideways foot. If there is another way, he takes it with care and does not go straight on; and so, with care, he wanders from village to village.

3.2.3 · Thrown into the water

से णेवं वयंतं परो सहसा बलसा बाहाहिं गहाय णावाओ उदगंसि पक्खिवेज्जा, तं णो सुमणे सिया, णो दुम्मणे सिया, णो उच्चावयं मणं णियच्छेज्जा, णो तेसिं बालाणं घायाए वहाए समुट्ठेज्जा । अप्पुस्सुए जाव समाहीए । तओ संजयामेव उदगंसि पवेज्जा ।

If, even while he says this, someone suddenly and by force seizes him by the arms and throws him from the boat into the water, he should be neither pleased nor displeased, should not let his mind swing high and low, and should not rise up to hurt or kill those ignorant people. Free of agitation, steady in calm absorption, he should float carefully in the water.

3.3.7 · Silence before the hunter

से भिक्खू वा भिक्खुणी वा गामाणुगामं दूइज्जमाणे अंतरा से पाडिपहिया आगच्छेज्जा, ते णं पाडिपहिया एवं वएज्जा- आउसंतो समणा ! अवियाइं एत्तो पडिपहे पासह, तं जहा- मणुस्सं वा गोणं वा महिसं वा पसुं वा पक्खिं वा सरीसवं वा जलयरं वा, से तं मे आइक्खह, दंसेह । तं णो आइक्खेज्जा, णो दंसेज्जा, णो तस्स तं परिण्णं परिजाणेज्जा, तुसिणीओ उवेहेज्जा, जाणं वा णो जाणं ति वएज्जा । तओ संजयामेव गामाणुगामं दूइजेज्जा ।

If travellers on the road ask, “Long-lived monk, did you see along this way a man, an ox, a buffalo, an animal, a bird, a reptile or a water creature? Tell us, show us,” he should not tell, should not show, should not take up the request, but stay silent; or, though he knows, he may say “I do not know.” Then he should wander on with care. The commentary gives two readings of the last phrase: in hard moments, to protect the animals, he says he does not know; normally, he simply does not say that he knows, and stays silent.

Note: the print ends this sutra with दूइजेज्जा, where every other sutra has दूइज्जेज्जा; it looks like a misprint, kept as printed.

3.3.15 · Robbed, he complains to no one

ते णं आमोसगा सयं करणिज्जं ति कट्टु अक्कोसंति वा जाव उद्दवेंति वा, वत्थं वा पायं वा कंबलं वा पायपुंछणं वा आच्छिंदेज्ज वा अवहरेज्ज वा, परिट्ठवेज्ज वा, तं णो गामसंसारियं कुज्जा, णो रायसंसारियं कुज्जा, णो परं उवसंकमित्तु बूया- आउसंतो गाहावई ! एए खलु आमोसगा उवगरणपडियाए सयं करणिज्जं ति कट्टु अक्कोसंति वा जाव परिट्ठवेंति वा । एयप्पगारं मणं वा वइं वा णो पुरओ कट्टु विहरेज्जा । अप्पुस्सुए जाव समाहीए तओ संजयामेव गामाणुगामं दूइज्जेज्जा ।

If the robbers, doing what robbers do, abuse him, beat him or try to kill him, and snatch, carry off or throw away his cloth, bowl, blanket or foot-wiper, he should not report it to the village or to the king, nor go to a householder and say, “Long-lived householder, these robbers abused me and threw away my things.” He should not even give such a thought room in mind or speech, but, free of agitation and steady in calm, wander on with care.
