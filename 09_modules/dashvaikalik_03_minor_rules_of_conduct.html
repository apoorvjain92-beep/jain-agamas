Dashvaikalik 3 · Kshullakachara Katha (क्षुल्लकाचार कथा): Minor Rules of Conduct

In one line: a monk who wants to be free keeps away from a long list of small-looking comforts and habits (the tradition counts fifty-two), because each one brings harm to living beings, attachment to the body or dependence on householders; by keeping clear of them he burns off his karma.

Source: Dashvaikalik Sutra, chapter 3 (15 verses), translated pages 95–121 (data/translations/dashvaikalik/). 14 minutes.

What the chapter teaches

  1. Why “minor” rules. Chapter 6 gives the monk’s conduct at length; this chapter gives it in short, by listing what must not be done, so it is called the account of minor (kshullaka) conduct. Conduct is the heart of knowledge, and keeping it pure helps the monk and, the introduction adds, brings peace to society and the world (introduction, p. 95).
  2. Who the rules are for. These things are not done by the great sages, the nirgranthas (those free of every knot of possession), who are firmly set in restraint, free of worldly ties, and protectors of themselves and of all six kinds of living beings. Each quality depends on the others (v.1).
  3. Rules about food. No food made for the monk, bought for him, taken daily from a house that invited him, or carried to him from elsewhere. No food that is stored, eaten from a householder’s dish, prepared for a king, or made after asking “What would you like?” No food from the family that gives him lodging, and no food or water not fully free of life. Behind most of these lies one reason: cooking and buying for a monk harm living beings, and accepting it makes him a party to that harm (v.2–3, 5–6).
  4. No eating at night. For a nirgrantha, giving up night food is part of the great vow. Bringing food by day to eat at night, by night to eat by day, by day to eat the next day, or by night to eat at night are all forbidden (v.2, commentary).
  5. No care and decoration of the body. No bathing, scent, garlands, fanning, massage, cleaning or colouring the teeth, looking at oneself in a mirror, collyrium for the eyes, oiling or adorning the body, or purging and cleansing treatments done for the body’s sake. Adornment, says the commentary, endangers celibacy and binds sticky karma (v.2–3, 5, 9).
  6. No comforts and no worldly ties. No games of dice, no umbrella, no footwear, no lighting fire, no sitting on chairs or beds, no sitting in a householder’s home, no serving householders or asking about their affairs, no getting food by mentioning one’s caste, family, craft, learning or austerity, and no calling on family for refuge when in hardship. The commentary notes allowed exceptions, such as an umbrella or treatment in serious illness, set against the general rule (v.3–6).
  7. Nothing living. Radish, ginger, sugarcane pieces, tubers, roots, fruits and seeds while still living, and six kinds of living (unprocessed) salt, are not to be taken (v.7–8).
  8. The true nirgrantha. He knows and stops the five inflows of karma (ashrava), guards mind, speech and body, protects the six kinds of beings, holds the five senses in check, is steady, and looks only to the straight path. He bears the summer sun, goes without covering in winter, and stays in one place during the rains to protect the many beings born then (v.10–12).
  9. The goal: an end to all suffering. Having conquered hardships as enemies, shaken off delusion and won over the senses, the great sages strive to end all suffering. After doing what is hard and bearing what is hard to bear, some go to the heavens and some, free of karma’s dust, reach liberation (v.13–14).
  10. Restraint closes the inlet, austerity dries the pond. Through restraint and austerity they destroy their past karma, reach the path to liberation and are fully freed. The commentary’s picture: first close the channels feeding a pond, then the sun dries up what is left (v.15).

Key verses

1 · For whom these things are wrong

संजमे सुट्ठिअप्पाणं, विप्पमुक्काण ताइणं ।
तेसिमेयमणाइण्णं णिग्गंथाण महेसिणं ॥

For the great sages, the nirgranthas, whose souls are well set in restraint, who are free of all ties and protect themselves and others, the following things are not to be done.

2 · The first nine wrong practices

उद्देसियं कीयगडं, णियागमभिहडाणि य ।
राइभत्ते सिणाणे य, गंधमल्ले य वीयणे ॥

Food made for the monk, food bought for him, food taken daily from a house by invitation, and food brought to him; eating at night, bathing, scents, garlands and fanning.

11 · Marks of the nirgrantha

पंचासवपरिण्णाया, तिगुत्ता छसु संजया ।
पंचणिग्गहणा धीरा, णिग्गंथा उज्जुदंसिणो ॥

They know the five inflows and give them up, guard themselves in three ways, are careful toward the six kinds of beings, hold the five senses in check, are steady, and see the straight path: these are the nirgranthas.

12 · Through the three seasons

आयावयंति गिम्हेसु, हेमंतेसु अवाउडा ।
वासासु पडिसंलीणा, संजया सुसमाहिया ॥

In summer they stand in the sun’s heat, in winter they stay uncovered, in the rains they stay withdrawn in one place: restrained and well collected.

13 · Striving to end all suffering

परीसह रिऊदंता, धुयमोहा जिइंदिया ।
सव्वदुक्खप्पहीणट्ठा, पक्कमंति महेसिणो ॥

Having tamed hardships as enemies, shaken off delusion and conquered the senses, the great sages strive to put an end to all suffering.

15 · Freed by restraint and austerity

खवित्ता पुव्वकमाइं, संजमेण तवेण य ।
सिद्धिमग्गमणुप्पत्ता, ताइणो परिणिव्वुडा ॥ त्ति बेमि ॥

Having destroyed their past karma through restraint and austerity, and having reached the path to liberation, the protectors of all beings are fully freed. Thus I say.

(As printed: पुव्वकमाइं. The same page’s word meanings read पुव्वकम्माइं, “past karmas”, so this is a misprint; the English follows the meaning.)
