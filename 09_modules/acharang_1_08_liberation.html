Acharang 1.8 · Vimoksha (વિમોક્ષ): Liberation

In one line: a monk frees himself step by step, from loose company, from food and lodging made for him, from clothes, from the help of others and from the taste of food, until, when the body can no longer carry restraint, he gives it up calmly in one of three ways of dying in equanimity, wanting neither life nor death.

Source: Acharang Sutra, Book 1 (First Shrutaskandha), chapter 8 (30 sutras in sections 1–7 and 25 verses in section 8, 8 sections), translated pages 331–397 (data/translations/acharang-1/). The book numbers the sutras afresh in each section, so references below read section.sutra (8.2.3 = section 2, sutra 3). 16 minutes.

What the chapter teaches

  1. Liberation (vimoksha) means release from delusion and karma. The introduction explains that release from a shackle is material release; release from the passions and from karma is spiritual release. It grows by degrees, from the person with right faith to the monk, and is complete in the perfected ones. The three ways of dying in equanimity (bhakta-pratyakhyana, ingita-marana, padapopagamana) are also called liberation because they lead to it (introduction, pp.331–332).
  2. Keep dealings over food and requisites few. A monk should not, without a purpose and with great show of regard, give food, clothes, bowls or service to other monks outside his own eating circle. The commentary says this is to cut down outward business, not to breed dislike; for a sick, weak or helpless monk, giving and serving are allowed (8.1.1, 8.2.4). To a well-conducted monk of the same observance he gives and serves with respect (8.2.5).
  3. Do not accept invitations from ascetics of other faiths, and do not be caught in one-sided views. Some ascetics harm beings, take what is not given, and argue that the world is eternal or not, has an end or not, that there is hell or no hell, each claiming his own way frees from karma. Their teaching is not well stated as the Lord’s is. With a quarrelsome opponent the monk guards his speech or keeps silent (8.1.2–4).
  4. Dharma is giving up sin, in any place and at any age. All views accept some sin; going beyond it is the great discernment. Dharma is “neither in the village nor in the forest” but in the soul, and people have woken up in all three stages of life. So the wise one neither harms the six kinds of living beings, nor has them harmed, nor approves of it (8.1.5–6).
  5. Refuse food or lodging made for you, even if it costs you a beating. If a householder cooks, buys, borrows or seizes food, or builds a dwelling, for the monk, the monk politely refuses: “I have renounced such activity.” If the giver, angered, strikes him or orders him beaten, the steadfast monk endures it, and then either explains monks’ conduct to someone ready to listen or stays silent (8.2.1–3).
  6. Take only what the body needs for restraint, and do not warm yourself at a fire. Some take up the monk’s life in middle age, the age the commentary calls generally the best for it. The body grows by food and is broken by hardship, so the monk knows the right time, his strength and the right measure (8.3.1–2). A monk shivering with cold tells the kind householder that the senses do not trouble him, he simply cannot bear the cold, yet he may not light a fire or have one lit for him (8.3.3).
  7. Fewer clothes, more lightness. Sections 4 to 7 describe monks who vow to keep three garments, two, one, or none but a waist-cloth. Such a monk does not ask for one more, does not wash or dye his cloth, and as summer comes he lets worn-out cloth go. “Attaining lightness, he becomes endowed with austerity” is the refrain of every vow (8.4.1–2, 8.5.1–2, 8.6.1, 8.7.1–2).
  8. A hard exception, stated for the sake of celibacy. Section 4 says that if a monk is trapped by a woman’s advances and cannot otherwise keep his vow, it is better for him to give up his life than to break celibacy, and calls that death timely and freeing. The commentary explains it as an emergency exception, not the rule; the rule is the slow, prepared death of the wise (8.4.3, commentary pp.361–362).
  9. Stand on your own: refuse food brought to you, choose how you will give and take service, know you are alone, and do not chase taste. A weak monk still refuses food a householder brings to his lodging (8.5.3). Monks may vow, in four set ways, whether they will fetch food for fellow monks and whether they will accept it; illness and spare food allow set exceptions (8.5.4, 8.7.3). A monk who thinks “I am alone; no one is mine and I am no one’s” knows himself as alone (8.6.2). He does not move a morsel from cheek to cheek to savour it (8.6.3).
  10. When the body fails, three ways of dying in equanimity. First the monk reduces food and thins the passions (sallekhana), then he lies on a grass bed in a clean, lifeless place. In bhakta-pratyakhyana he gives up food (8.5.5); in ingita-marana he also refuses all help and moves only inside a fixed area (8.6.4, 8.8.12–18); in padapopagamana, the highest, he lies still like a tree, renouncing body, activity and movement (8.7.4, 8.8.19–25). Section 8 puts it in verse: do not long to live or to die, let creatures feed on the body without driving them off, see that “the hardships are in the body, not in me”, and refuse even the pleasures a god may offer (8.8.1–25).

Key passages

8.1.5 · Dharma is neither in the village nor in the forest

सव्वत्थ सम्मयं पावं । तमेव उवाइकम्म, एस महं विवेगे वियाहिए । गामे अदुवा रण्णे, णेव गामे णेव रण्णे, धम्ममायाणह पवेइयं माहणेण मइमया । जामा तिण्णि उदाहिया जेसु इमे आयरिया संबुज्झमाणा समुट्ठिया, जे णिव्वुडा पावेहिं कम्मेहिं अणियाणा ते वियाहिया ।

Sin is accepted in some form by every view; going beyond it is the great discernment that has been declared. Dharma can be practised in a village or in a forest, yet it is neither in the village nor in the forest: know the dharma taught by the all-knowing Lord. Three stages of life are named, in each of which noble ones have woken up and taken up restraint. Those who have turned away from sinful deeds are called free of karma.

8.4.2 · Letting the worn-out cloth go

अह पुण एवं जाणेज्जा–उवाइक्कंते खलु हेमंते, गिम्हे पडिवण्णे, से अहापरिजुण्णाइं वत्थाइं परिट्ठवेज्जा, अदुवा संतरुत्तरे, अदुवा ओमचेलिए, अदुवा एगसाडे, अदुवा अचेले । लाघवियं आगममाणे । तवे से अभिसमण्णागए भवइ। जहेयं भगवया पवेइयं तमेव अभिसमेच्चा सव्वओ सव्वत्ताए सम्मत्तमेव समभिजाणिज्जा ।

When he knows that winter has passed and summer has come, he should lay aside the garments that have worn out: keeping three, or fewer, or a single cloth, or none. Attaining lightness, he gains austerity as well. Understanding this just as the Lord taught it, he should know it rightly, fully and in every way.

8.6.2 · I am alone

जस्स णं भिक्खुस्स एवं भवइ– एगो अहमंसि, ण मे अत्थि कोइ, ण याहमवि कस्सइ । एवं से एगागिणमेव अप्पाणं समभिजाणेज्जा । लाघवियं आगममाणे तवे से अभिसमण्णागए भवइ । जहेयं भगवया पवेइयं तमेव अभिसमेच्चा सव्वओ सव्वत्ताए सम्मत्तमेव समभिजाणिज्जा ।

A monk to whom this thought comes, “I am alone; no one is mine, and I am no one’s”, should know himself as truly alone. Attaining lightness, he gains austerity. Understanding this just as the Lord taught it, he should know it rightly, fully and in every way.

8.8.4 · Want neither life nor death

जीवियं णाभिकंखेज्जा, मरणं णो वि पत्थए ।
दुहओ वि ण सज्जेज्जा, जीविए मरणे तहा ॥

He should not long to live, nor wish to die. He should cling to neither, life or death.

8.8.5 · Seek the purity within

मज्झत्थो णिज्जरापेही, समाहिमणुपालए ।
अंतो बहिं विउसिज्ज, अज्झत्थं सुद्धमेसए ॥

Even-minded and intent on shedding karma, he should keep his inner composure (samadhi). Letting go of what is inside (the passions) and what is outside (the body and its things), he should seek the purity of the inner self.

8.8.21 · The hardships are in the body, not in me

अचित्तं तु समासज्ज, ठावए तत्थ अप्पगं ।
वोसिरे सव्वसो कायं, ण मे देहे परीसहा ॥

Having reached a place free of living beings, he should settle himself there and give up the body completely, thinking: “These hardships are in the body; they are not mine.”
