Dashvaikalik 2 · Shramanyapurvaka (सामण्णपुव्वयं): The Groundwork of Monkhood

In one line: no one can live as a monk without first giving up desire for sense pleasures, and when the mind slips, as Rathanemi’s did, it must be brought back at once, as Rajimati brought him back with a few hard words.

Source: Dashvaikalik Sutra, chapter 2 (11 verses), translated pages 76–94 (data/translations/dashvaikalik/). 12 minutes.

What the chapter teaches

  1. Giving up desire comes before monkhood. The name means “what comes before the monk’s life”. Its seed is turning away from desire; the commentator adds patience, since only a patient person can drop desire (introduction, p. 76).
  2. A man ruled by desire sinks at every step. How can someone who does not hold back desire live as a monk? Caught in his own plans and longings, he grows downcast at every step. Desire and restraint are like light and dark: they cannot stay together. The commentary quotes: “Desire, I know you; you are born of thought. I will not think of you, so you will not arise.” It tells of a young monk who asked his father-guru for one comfort after another (shoes, socks, a head cloth, a soft bed, a bath) until he finally said he could not live without a woman, and the guru left him (v.1).
  3. Not having is not giving up. A man who does not enjoy clothes, scents, ornaments, women and beds only because he cannot get them is not called a renouncer. The true renouncer turns his back on lovely, wanted pleasures that are in his hands, by his own choice. Two stories make the point: Subandhu, who lived like a monk only out of fear of a poisoned scent left by Chanakya, was no renouncer; and when Abhayakumar offered three heaps of gems to anyone who would give up water, fire and women for life, no one took them, which showed that the woodcutter-monk had given up more than gems (v.2–3).
  4. When the mind wanders: “She is not mine, nor am I hers.” Even a careful monk’s mind may stray. He should remove the attachment with this thought, seeing the woman and every other thing as separate from the soul. The commentary’s picture: when a prince’s pebble cracks a servant girl’s water pot, she plugs it with wet clay at once; plug a bad thought the same way (v.4).
  5. Four remedies. Bear heat (practise austerity), give up softness and comfort, get past desire (and suffering is gone with it), cut off aversion and remove attachment. Do this and you will be happy (v.5).
  6. The story behind the verses. Prince Arishtanemi turned back from his own wedding when he saw animals penned to be killed for the feast, and became a monk. Rajimati, his bride-to-be, also renounced. On her way to Girnar a storm separated her from the other nuns; she entered a cave and spread out her wet clothes. Rathanemi, Arishtanemi’s younger brother and a monk, was meditating there, saw her in a flash of lightning, and his mind turned to desire (introduction, pp. 77–78).
  7. The snake that will not take back its poison. Snakes of the Agandhana family would rather enter a blazing fire than suck back poison they have spat out. “Shame on you, seeker of disgrace, who would drink back what you vomited for the sake of this life. Death would be better” (v.6–7).
  8. Remember who you are. Rajimati reminds him that she is King Ugrasena’s daughter and he is of the Andhaka-Vrishni line: let us not be like the Gandhana snake; steady your mind and live in restraint. If you long for every woman you see, you will drift like the rootless hada plant pushed by the wind (v.8–9).
  9. He came back, as an elephant to the goad. Hearing her well-spoken words, Rathanemi was set firmly back in dharma. The commentary says her words worked because her own conduct was pure. Both later reached liberation (v.10; p. 78).
  10. The wise do the same. The awakened, learned and discerning turn away from pleasures just as that best of men did. The commentary explains why he is still called “best of men”: one who slips but admits the fault, takes good advice and returns to restraint is great; best of all is one who never wavers (v.11).

Key verses

1 · Desire first, or no monkhood

कहं णु कुज्जा सामण्णं, जो कामे ण णिवारए ।
पए पए विसीयंतो, संकप्पस्स वसं गओ ॥

How can one live as a monk who does not hold back desire? Under the sway of his own longings, he sinks into gloom at every step.

3 · The true renouncer

जे य कंते पिए भोए, लद्धे वि पिट्ठि कुव्वइ ।
साहीणे चयइ भोए, से हु चाइ त्ति वुच्चइ ॥

The one who turns his back on lovely and dear pleasures even when he has them, and gives up pleasures that are in his own power, is truly called a renouncer.

4 · She is not mine, nor am I hers

समाइ पेहाइ परिव्वयंतो, सिया मणो णिस्सरइ बहिद्धा ।
ण सा महं णो वि अहं पि तीसे, इच्चेव ताओ विणएज्ज रागं ॥

If, while a monk moves about with care, his mind should wander outside restraint, let him remove his attachment to her with this thought: “She is not mine, nor am I hers.”

6 · The Agandhana snake

पक्खंदे जलियं जोइं, धूमकेउं दुरासयं ।
णेच्छंति वंतयं भोत्तुं, कुले जाया अगंधणे ॥

Snakes born in the Agandhana family leap into a blazing, smoky fire that is hard even to approach, but they will not take back what they have vomited.

7 · Rajimati’s rebuke

धिरत्थु तेऽजसोकामी, जो तं जीवियकारणा ।
वंतं इच्छसि आवेउं, सेयं ते मरणं भवे ॥

Shame on you, seeker of disgrace, who for the sake of this life want to drink back what you have vomited. Death would be better for you.

10 · Like an elephant to the goad

तीसे सो वयणं सोच्चा, संजयाइ सुभासियं ।
अंकुसेण जहा णागो, धम्मे संपडिवाइओ ॥

Hearing the well-spoken words of that restrained nun, he was brought back to dharma, as an elephant is brought back by the goad.
