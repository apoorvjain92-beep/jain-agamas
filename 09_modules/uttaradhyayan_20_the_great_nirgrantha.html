Uttaradhyayan 20 · Mahanirgranthiya: The Great Nirgrantha

In one line: no wealth, family or doctor can protect us from suffering; only the soul that takes up restraint and keeps it truly has a protector, and a monk who lets his restraint slip becomes unprotected again.

Source: Uttaradhyayan Sutra Part 1, chapter 20 (60 verses), translated pages 480–507 (data/translations/uttaradhyayan-1/). 12 minutes.

What the chapter teaches

  1. A teaching on what is real, how to practise, and where it leads. The chapter opens with homage to the Siddhas and the self-restrained, and promises a true teaching on the nature of things, on dharma, and on liberation (v.1).
  2. A king meets a young monk. King Shrenik of Magadha, out on a walk in the Mandikukshi garden, sees a young, delicate monk deep in meditation under a tree. Amazed by his beauty, calm and detachment, he bows and asks why he became a monk at the age for pleasure (v.2–8).
  3. “I had no protector.” The monk answers that he was without a protector (anatha) and found no friend to care for him. The king laughs and offers to be his protector, so that he can enjoy life among friends, “for human birth is rare” (v.9–11).
  4. “You have none yourself.” The monk replies that the king, being without a protector himself, cannot protect anyone. The king, stunned, lists his horses, elephants, city, palace and power; the monk says he does not know what “protector” really means (v.12–17).
  5. The monk’s story: no one could take the pain away. In Kaushambi, the son of a very rich man, he was struck in youth by burning pain in his eyes and body, like an enemy’s sharp weapon. The best healers gave fourfold treatment; his father would have given all his wealth; his mother, brothers and sisters tried; his wife wept at his side, did not eat or bathe, and never left him. None could free him from suffering: “this was my helplessness” (v.18–30).
  6. The resolve, and the cure. Seeing that he would have to bear such pain again and again in endless worldly life, he resolved: if freed from this pain even once, he would become forgiving, self-controlled, free of harmful activity, and a homeless monk. He fell asleep; the pain faded through the night; next morning he took leave of his family and became a monk (v.31–34).
  7. The soul is its own protector, friend and enemy. By taking initiation he became the protector of himself, of others, and of all moving and still beings. The soul itself is hell’s Vaitarani river and thorn tree, and also the wish-granting cow and the Nandana grove; it makes and unmakes its own joy and sorrow (v.35–37). The commentary: one who can protect himself and others has a protector; the means are to see that nothing outside can remove suffering, to reflect on karma, and to resolve on renunciation.
  8. The second kind of helplessness: the slack monk. Some take the Nirgrantha path and then grow lax: they do not keep the great vows with care, are greedy for tastes, careless in walking, speaking, seeking alms, handling things and disposing of waste, live off the monk’s dress, tell fortunes from marks, dreams and omens, and accept any food at all, “all-consuming like fire”. Such a one is an empty fist, a counterfeit coin, glass passing as a gem; his dharma, joined to sense-pleasure, kills like poison, a weapon held wrongly, or an untamed spirit. He loses both this world and the next (v.38–50).
  9. Leave the bad path, walk the great one. The wise one who hears this abandons the way of the ill-conducted, keeps the highest restraint, stops the inflow of karma, destroys karma, and reaches the eternal, supreme place (v.51–53).
  10. The king is won over. Shrenik says the monk has explained being without a protector exactly as it is; the monk’s human birth is truly fruitful and he truly has a protector and kin. He asks forgiveness for disturbing his meditation and tempting him with pleasures, and with his household and kin becomes devoted to the dharma. The monk wanders on, free as a bird, rid of delusion (v.54–60).

Key verses

12 · “You are without a protector yourself”

अप्पणा वि अणाहो सि, सेणिया मगहाहिवा ।
अप्पणा अणाहो संतो, कहं णाहो भविस्ससि ॥

O Shrenik, lord of Magadha, you yourself are without a protector. Being without a protector yourself, how can you be anyone else’s protector?

32 · The resolve in the night

सइं च जइ मुच्चेज्जा, वेयणा विउला इओ ।
खंतो दंतो णिरारंभो, पव्वइए अणगारियं ॥

If I am freed even once from this terrible pain, I will become forgiving, master of my senses, free of harmful activity, and take up the homeless life.

35 · Protector of all beings

तओऽहं णाहो जाओ, अप्पणो य परस्स य ।
सव्वेसिं चेव भूयाणं, तसाणं थावराण य ॥

Then I became a protector: of myself and of others, and of all living beings, moving and still.

36 · The soul is hell and heaven to itself

अप्पा णई वेयरणी, अप्पा मे कूडसामली ।
अप्पा कामदुहा धेणू, अप्पा मे णंदणं वणं ॥

The soul itself is my Vaitarani river and my Kutasalmali tree (the torments of hell); the soul itself is the wish-granting cow and my Nandana grove.

37 · The soul is its own friend and enemy

अप्पा कत्ता विकत्ता य, दुहाण य सुहाण य ।
अप्पा मित्तममित्तं च, दुप्पट्ठिय-सुपट्ठिओ ॥

The soul itself makes and unmakes its sorrows and joys. The soul set on the right path is its own friend; set on the wrong path, its own enemy.

48 · Worse than an enemy with a knife

ण तं अरी कंठछेत्ता करेइ, जं से करे अप्पणिया दुरप्पा ।
से णाहइ मच्चुमुहं तु पत्ते, पच्छाणुतावेण दयाविहूणो ॥

An enemy who cuts one’s throat cannot do the harm that one’s own soul does when it goes wrong. The one without compassion realises this only at the mouth of death, in regret.
