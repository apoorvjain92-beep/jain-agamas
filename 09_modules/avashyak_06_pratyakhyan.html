Avashyak 6 · Pratyakhyan (પ્રત્યાખ્યાન): Vows of Renunciation

In one line: having cleaned out past faults, the practitioner closes the door on future karma by taking a time-bound vow to give up food (from one muhurta after sunrise up to a full fast), keeps it pure, closes it properly, and ends the whole Avashyak with the Namotthunam in praise of the Arihantas and Siddhas.

Source: Avashyak Sutra, 6th Avashyak and the closing Namotthunam (10 lessons: 10 renunciation formulas, the closing formula, 1 Niryukti verse, and the Namotthunam), translated pages 201–229. 18 minutes.

What the chapter teaches

  1. Renunciation stops what is still to come. The 4th and 5th Avashyaks destroy karma already gathered; the 6th, pratyakhyan, blocks karma that would come in future. It is a firm resolve, made with right knowledge, to give up sinful activity. The commentary sorts vows into root vows (the five great vows of a monk, the five small vows of a layman) and supporting vows, and lists ten kinds of supporting vow; this Avashyak teaches the tenth, the time-bound vows (addha pratyakhyan). The Anuyogadvara Sutra calls this Avashyak “holding on to virtues” (gunadharana) (introduction, p.201).
  2. Navakarshi: the shortest vow. “When the sun has risen” one gives up all four kinds of food (staple food, water, fruit and sweets, and mouth-fresheners) until one muhurta after sunrise has passed and the Navkar has been said. Both conditions are needed. It has only two exceptions (agara): complete forgetfulness and a sudden accident, like a drop of buttermilk splashing into the mouth. The same words, with “a knot” or “a fist” in place of the Navkar, make a vow that ends when one unties the knot or opens the fist (l.1, pp.202–204).
  3. Porisi and two-porisi: the exceptions show the aim. Porisi lasts one prahar, until a man’s shadow equals his height; it adds exceptions for a clouded sky, confusing the directions, and a trusted person’s word. The exception “for the sake of complete equanimity” lets a sudden severe pain be treated, because a vow that produces distress and anger has already lost its purpose. The two-porisi vow (purimaddha) adds “by order of an elder”: serving a sick monk is an inner austerity, greater than the outer austerity of fasting (l.2–3, pp.204–206).
  4. Eating once. Ekasana is one meal a day, after which only boiled water (or nothing) is taken; it has eight exceptions, two of which (a householder arriving, and eating leftover alms that would otherwise be thrown away) are for monks only. Ekatthana is stricter: one meal without moving any limb except the right hand and the mouth, so it has seven exceptions (l.4–5, pp.206–209).
  5. Ayambil conquers the tongue. One meal of dry, bland food such as boiled rice or urad, with no rich foods (vigai: milk, curd, ghee, oil, jaggery, sugar). It ranks above ekasana because in ekasana one may still eat all six tastes. Its extra exceptions cover a trace of ghee left on the giver’s ladle, and similar cases; these are for monks, whose food is given by others (l.6, pp.209–211).
  6. The fast runs from sunrise to sunrise. A fast (upavas) gives up all four foods, or three if water is kept; on a three-food fast six kinds of water are allowed. Names like “fourth meal” for a one-day fast are only old conventional names (l.7, pp.211–212).
  7. The end of the day, the end of life, and a secret resolve. Divas-charim gives up food from evening until the next sunrise; bhav-charim is the same vow taken for the rest of life (santhara). An abhigraha is a private condition: “I will eat only when such-and-such happens, otherwise I will fast so many days.” It is told to no one, and should be taken only after honestly weighing one’s own strength, not to copy others (l.8–9, pp.212–214).
  8. Giving up rich foods. Nirvigai gives up ghee, oil, milk, curd, jaggery and sugar, with nine exceptions. A table in the book shows the count for each vow: Navakarshi 2, Porisi 6, two-porisi 7, ekasana 8, ekatthana 7, ayambil 8, fast 5, divas-charim 4, nirvigai 9, abhigraha 4 (l.10, pp.214–215).
  9. Six purities, and closing the vow properly. A vow must be pure in faith, knowledge, respect, recitation, keeping and inner feeling. When it ends, the closing formula declares it “touched, kept, carried to the end, praised, purified and fulfilled”, and asks that any failure be undone (pp.216–217).
  10. The Namotthunam ends the Avashyak. The text opened with the Navkar, put the Logassa in the middle, and ends with this praise. Each word names a virtue: the Tirthankara builds a ford (tirtha) across worldly life in the fourfold sangha; he is a lamp, not a sun, because a lamp lights other lamps and makes them like itself; he gives fearlessness, the greatest gift; and he has reached the Siddha state, which is free of trouble, unmoving, without end and without return. Said for the Arihanta, the ending changes to “who wishes to reach that place”; a third Namotthunam is said to one’s own teacher. It is also called Shakrastava, because Indra praises the Lord with it (pp.218–229).

Key passages

Lesson 1, s.1 · The Navakarshi vow

उग्गए सूरे णमुक्कारसहियं पच्चक्खामि चउव्विहं पि आहारं-असणं पाणं खाइमं साइमं अण्णत्थणाभोगेणं सहसागारेणं वोसिरामि ।

Now that the sun has risen, I take the vow that ends with the Navkar: I give up all four kinds of food (staple food, water, fruit and sweets, mouth-fresheners), except through forgetfulness or sudden accident. I let them go.

Lesson 7, s.1 · The fast

उग्गए सूरे, अभत्तट्ठं पच्चक्खामि, चउव्विहं पि आहारं-असणं, पाणं, खाइमं, साइमं । अण्णत्थणाभोगेणं, सहसागारेणं, पारिट्ठावणियागारेणं, महत्तरागारेणं, सव्वसमाहिवत्तियागारेणं, वोसिरमि ।

Now that the sun has risen, I take the vow of “no purpose of food” (a fast) and give up all four kinds of food, except through forgetfulness, sudden accident, leftover alms that would be thrown away, an elder’s order, or to keep complete equanimity. I let them go.

Niryukti 1585 (no. 2) · Six purities of a vow

सा पुन सद्दहण जाणणा य विनयानुभासणा चेव ।
अनुपालना विसोही भावविसोही भवे छट्ठा ॥

Its purity lies in faith, knowledge, respect and correct recitation, in keeping it, and sixth, in purity of inner feeling.

No. 3 · Closing the vow

उग्गएसूरे नमुक्कार सहियं… पच्चक्खाणं कयं । तं पच्चक्खाणं सम्मं काएण फासियं, पालियं, तीरियं, किट्टियं, सोहियं, आराहियं । जं च न आराहियं तस्स मिच्छामि दुक्कडम् ।

At sunrise I took the vow that ends with the Navkar. That vow I have properly touched with the body, kept, carried to its end, praised, purified and fulfilled. Whatever I have not fulfilled, may that wrong of mine be undone.

Namotthunam, opening · Salutation to the Arihantas

णमोत्थुणं अरिहंताणं भगवंताणं आइगराणं तित्थयराणं सयंसंबुद्धाणं पुरिसुत्तमाणं पुरिस-सीहाणं पुरिस-वर-पुंडरियाणं पुरिसवर-गंधहत्थीणं लोगुत्तमाणं लोगणाहाणं लोगहियाणं लोगपईवाणं लोगपज्जोयगराणं अभयदयाणं …

Salutation to the Arihanta Bhagavans: the beginners of dharma, the founders of the ford, awakened by themselves; best among men, lions among men, white lotuses among men, scent-elephants among men; highest in the world, lords of the world, friends of the world, lamps of the world, lighters of the world; givers of fearlessness …

Namotthunam, close · Crossed over, and helping others cross

… जिणाणं-जावयाणं तिण्णाणं तारयाणं बुद्धाणं-बोहयाणं मुत्ताणं-मोयगाणं सव्वण्णूणं सव्वदरिसीणं सिव-मयल-मरुय-मणंत-मक्खय-मव्वाबाह-मपुणरावित्ति सिद्धिगइ णामधेयं ठाणं संपत्ताणं णमो जिणाणं जियभयाणं ।

… who have conquered and help others conquer, who have crossed and carry others across, who are awake and awaken others, who are free and free others; all-knowing and all-seeing; who have reached the place called the Siddha state, free of trouble, unmoving, free of disease, endless, imperishable, unhindered and without return. Salutation to the Jinas, who have conquered fear.

The print itself reads वोसिरमि at the end of l.7, where every other vow has वोसिरामि (“I let go”); kept as printed, probably a misprint.
