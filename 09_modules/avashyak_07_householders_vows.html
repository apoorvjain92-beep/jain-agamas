Avashyak 7 · Shravak Vrat (શ્રાવક વ્રત): The Householder’s Vows

In one line: a lay follower first turns from wrong belief to right faith, then takes twelve vows sized to household life (five small vows, three supporting vows, four training vows), learns the faults that weaken each one, and at life’s end may let go of the body in sallekhana.

Source: Avashyak Sutra, “The vows of a Shravaka” section after the 6th Avashyak (15 sutras and 3 Niryukti verses), translated pages 230–264. 22 minutes.

What the chapter teaches

  1. This section comes from the commentary, not the sutra. The sutra’s own lessons (Karemi Bhante and the rest) speak of a monk’s great vows; it does not set out the lay vows. Haribhadra’s commentary, inside the 6th Avashyak on renunciation, adds them, and the book follows it. The lay follower’s five small vows are “partial root vows”; the other seven are “partial supporting vows” (pp.230–231).
  2. Right faith comes first. A shravaka is “one who listens” to dharma. Some take vows (like Ananda); some have only right faith (like King Shrenika). Before any vow, the lay follower turns back from wrong belief and accepts right faith: from now on he does not bow to other sects’ teachers, gods or shrines as a religious act, and does not feed their ascetics as a religious duty. There are six exceptions (king, community, force, a god, elders, hard times for one’s living), and giving out of compassion is not forbidden. The five faults of right faith are doubt, wanting another faith, doubting the fruit of dharma, praising other sects’ teachings, and close religious intimacy with them. Asking questions to strengthen faith, as Gautama did, is not doubt (s.1, pp.231–234).
  3. Non-violence, sized for a household. The layman gives up, for life, intentional violence; he cannot give up the violence of cooking, farming or travel. Tradition puts a monk’s compassion at 20 parts and a layman’s at 1¼: he renounces only intentional, purposeless violence to innocent moving beings. Vows can be taken in 49 combinations of doing, causing and approving by mind, speech and body. The faults are tying up, beating, cutting, overloading and withholding food, and today these include overworking servants and paying wages late (s.2, pp.234–239).
  4. No big lies; no theft. The layman gives up five gross lies: about a bride or groom, cattle, land, a deposit left in trust, and false witness. He gives up stealing by breaking in, opening locks, robbery or picking up what is plainly another’s; its faults include buying stolen goods, smuggling, false weights and passing off bad goods as good. The commentary keeps one test throughout: done by mistake it is a fault (atichara), done on purpose it breaks the vow (anachara) (s.3–4, pp.239–243).
  5. Content with one’s own spouse; a limit on possessions. The fourth vow keeps a man to his own wife (and a woman to her own husband). The fifth sets a limit on nine kinds of outer possessions: wealth, grain, fields, houses, silver, gold, people and animals in one’s keeping, and household goods. Possession is really attachment (murchha); anything that comes beyond the limit should be given away (s.5–6, pp.243–246).
  6. Three supporting vows guard the five. Limit how far you travel in each direction, so no harm is done beyond it. Limit daily things (towels, oils, clothes, foods, vehicles, beds: 26 items), avoid uncooked living foods, and avoid fifteen trades that cause great harm, such as charcoal-making, forest-cutting, dealing in ivory, liquor, poison or weapons, and draining lakes. Give up purposeless harm: brooding and cruel thoughts, careless habits, handing out weapons, and urging others into sin (s.7–9, pp.247–255).
  7. Four training vows are practised again and again. Samayik: 48 minutes of equanimity, spoiled by a wandering mind, harsh speech, a restless body, forgetting one is in samayik, or doing it irregularly. Deshavagasik: shrinking the travel limit for a day, and not getting round it by sending people or signalling. Paushadh: a full day and night lived like a monk, fasting, with no bathing or adornment, celibate, and free of business. Atithi samvibhag: giving monks pure food and fourteen kinds of things, honestly earned, at the right time, with respect and joy (s.10–13, pp.255–262).
  8. Right faith is the root of it all. The five small vows and three supporting vows are taken for life; the four training vows for a set time. Right faith arises either by itself or through teaching. A layman’s vows are “like gold ingots”: he may take one, two or three as his strength allows, and need not take all twelve at once (s.14, pp.262–263).
  9. Sallekhana: the last practice, not suicide. When the body can no longer serve spiritual practice, the layman confesses, gives up the eighteen sins, the four foods and attachment to the body, and waits for death in equanimity. Its faults are wishing for a good next life, for heaven, for longer life, for quick death, or for pleasures. Suicide comes from anger, grief or despair; this “wise death” comes from renunciation, so the two are as different as earth and sky (s.15, pp.263–264).

Key passages

s.1 · Turning to right faith

तत्थ समणोवासओ पुव्वामेव मिच्छत्ताओ पडिक्कमइ, सम्मत्तं उवसंपज्जइ, … सम्मत्तस्स समणोवासएणं इमे पंच अइयारा जाणियव्वा ण समायरियव्वा, तं जहा- संका, कंखा, वितिगिच्छा, परपासंडपसंसा, परपासंडसंथवो ।

There, first of all, the lay follower turns back from wrong belief and takes up right faith. … The lay follower should know these five faults of right faith but not commit them: doubt, longing for another faith, doubting the fruit of dharma, praising other sects, and close association with them.

s.2 · The vow of non-violence

थूलगपाणाइवायं समणोवासओ पच्चक्खाइ, से पाणाइवाए दुविहे पण्णत्ते, तं जहा- संकप्पओ अ आरंभओ अ । तत्थ समणोवासओ संकप्पओ जावज्जीवाए पच्चक्खाइ, नो आरंभओ । थूलगपाणाइवायवेरमणस्स समणोवासएणं इमे पंच अइयारा जाणियव्वा, (ण समायरियव्वा) तं जहा- बंधे, वहे, छविच्छेए, अइभारे, भत्तपाणवुच्छेए ।

The lay follower gives up gross violence to living beings. That violence is of two kinds: intended and arising from work. Of these he gives up intended violence for life, not violence arising from work. He should know, but not commit, five faults of this vow: tying up, beating, cutting the skin, overloading, and withholding food and water.

s.6 · A limit on desire

अपरिमियपरिग्गहं समणोवासओ पच्चक्खाइ इच्छापरिमाणं उवसंपज्जइ, से परिग्गहे दुविहे पण्णत्ते, तं जहा- सचित्तपरिग्गहे अचित्तपरिग्गहे । इच्छापरिमाणस्स समणोवासएणं इमे पंच अइयारा जाणियव्वा, ण समायरिव्वा तं जहा- धणधाण्णपमाणाइक्कमे, खित्तवत्थुपमाणाइक्कमे, हिरण्णसुवण्णपमाणाइक्कमे, दुपयचउप्पयपमाणाइक्कमे, कुवियपमाणाइक्कमे।

The lay follower gives up unlimited possessions and takes up a limit on desire. Possessions are of two kinds, living and non-living. He should know, but not commit, five faults: going beyond the limit set for wealth and grain, for fields and houses, for silver and gold, for two-footed and four-footed beings, and for household goods.

s.10 · Samayik

सामाइयं णाम सावज्जजोगपरिवज्जणं णिरवज्जजोगपडिसेवणं च । सामाइयस्स समणोवासएणं इमे पंच अइयारा जाणियव्वा, (ण समायरियव्वा) तं जहा- मणदुप्पणिहाणे, वइदुप्पणिहाणे, कायदुप्पणिहाणे, सामाइयस्स सइअकरणया, सामाइयस्स अणवट्ठियस्स करणया ।

Samayik means giving up sinful activity and taking up blameless activity. Its five faults, to be known but not committed: misusing the mind, misusing speech, misusing the body, forgetting that one is in samayik, and doing samayik unsteadily.

s.14 · The root is right faith

इत्थं पुण समणोवासगधम्मे पंचाणुव्वयाइं तिण्णि गुणव्वयाइं आवकहियाइं, चत्तारि सिक्खावयाइं इत्तरियाइं । एयस्स पुणो समणोवासगधम्मस्स मूलवत्थुं सम्मत्तं, तं जहा- तं णिसग्गेण वा अभिगमेण वा पंच अइयारविसुद्धं अणुव्वयगुणव्वयाइं च अभिग्गहाअण्णेवि पडिमादओ विसेसकरणजोगा ।

So in the lay follower’s dharma, the five small vows and three supporting vows are for life, and the four training vows are for a time. The root of this dharma is right faith, which comes by itself or through learning. With right faith free of its five faults, and the small and supporting vows kept, other resolves and the lay follower’s stages (pratima) may be taken up for special purification.

s.15 · Sallekhana

अपच्छिमा मारणंतिया संलेहणाझुसणाराहणया । इमीसे समणोवासएणं इमे पंच अइयारा जाणियव्वा, (ण समायरियव्वा) तं जहा- इहलोगासंसप्पओगे, परलोगासंसप्पओगे, जीवियासंसप्पओगे, मरणासंसप्पओगे, कामभोगासंसप्पओगे ।

The last practice, at the approach of death: lovingly following sallekhana, the thinning of body and passions. Its five faults, to be known but not committed: wishing for this world, wishing for the next world, wishing to live, wishing to die, and wishing for pleasures.

Checked against the scans: s.1 · s.2 · s.6 · s.10 · s.14 · s.15. Corrected from the translated pages: मारणंतिया (not मारंतिया) in s.15, धणधाण्ण in s.6. The print reads समायरिव्वा in s.6 where every other vow has समायरियव्वा (“to be practised”); kept as printed, probably a misprint.
