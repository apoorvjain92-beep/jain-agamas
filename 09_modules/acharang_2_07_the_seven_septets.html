Acharang 2.8–14 · Sapta-saptika (સપ્ત સપ્તિકા): The Seven Septets

In one line: seven short chapters guard a monk’s everyday life from every side: where he may stand still, sit to study and relieve himself, how he must not go out of his way for sounds and sights, and why he must not let a householder, or even a fellow monk, wash, rub, adorn or treat his body.

Source: Acharang Sutra, Book 2 (Second Shrutaskandha), chapters 8–14, together called the “seven septets” (101 sutras in 7 chapters: ch.8 has 6, ch.9 has 4, ch.10 has 24, ch.11 has 20, ch.12 has 1, ch.13 has 43, ch.14 has 3), translated pages 317–361 (data/translations/acharang-2/). The book numbers the sutras afresh in each chapter, so references read chapter.sutra (10.23 = chapter 10, sutra 23). 15 minutes.

What the chapters teach

  1. A place for standing (ch.8, Sthana-saptika). A monk who takes a vow only to stand, never to sit or lie down, first finds a place free of eggs, living beings and cobwebs and not built or altered for monks, as the lodging chapter already laid down (8.1). He then takes one of four standing resolves (pratima), each stricter than the last: lean on a lifeless wall, stretch the limbs and step about a little; lean and stretch but not step about; only lean; and finally lean on nothing, move nothing, and stand with all care for the body and its hair and nails given up (8.2–5). Whichever he takes, he must not run down monks who have taken none (8.5).
  2. A place for sitting and study (ch.9, Nishidhika). The study ground (nishidhika, ground for self-study) must likewise be free of living beings and faultless (9.1–2). When two to five monks go there together they must not embrace, kiss, or scratch one another with teeth or nails; the commentary says a lonely place can feed curiosity as well as meditation, so the monk keeps his purpose before his eyes (9.3).
  3. Where a monk may relieve himself (ch.10, Uchchara-prasravana). If he has no wiping cloth he may ask a fellow monk for one (10.1). He must not use ground that has living beings on it, that was prepared or cleaned for monks, where householders move roots and greens, or that is raised, unsteady or on living earth (10.2–9). The commentary: he could fall, and he would harm living beings.
  4. Not in places people use or hold sacred (ch.10). The list is long: where grain or seeds are spread out, rubbish heaps, cracked or muddy ground, cooking places and animal sheds, places where people kill themselves, gardens and temples, city gates and crossroads, cremation grounds, river ghats, new mines and ploughed fields, vegetable plots and groves (10.10–22). The commentary gives the reasons: harm to water, plant and fire beings, people’s anger and disgust, and dishonour to the teaching.
  5. The right way (ch.10). He goes with a pot to a lonely spot where no one comes or watches and nothing lives, relieves himself into the pot, then carries it to lifeless, burnt or similar ground and empties it there with care (10.23). The commentary quotes the Uttaradhyayan’s ten marks of proper ground, and says that where the ground has no owner the monk takes the permission of Shakra (Indra) (pp. 334–335).
  6. Do not go after sounds (ch.11, Shabda-saptika). A monk hears drums, strings, cymbals and wind instruments, the sounds of lakes, forests, towns, gardens, gates and crossroads, animal sheds, animal fights, wedding songs, story-telling and dance halls, quarrels and wars, processions, crowded places and great festivals. He must not set out to go and hear any of them (11.1–18). The commentary: sense objects do not bind karma; attachment and aversion to them do (p. 339). The chapter closes by forbidding attachment to any sound at all, of this world or the next, heard or unheard (11.19).
  7. Do not go after sights (ch.12, Rupa-saptika). The same rule for the eye: garlands, dolls, figures, woodwork, paintings, jewel and ivory work, and everything listed for sounds except the musical instruments (12.1). The commentary compares the monk drawn by beauty to the moth drawn into the flame (p. 347).
  8. Do not accept a householder’s bodily service (ch.13, Parakriya-saptika). Knowing that service done to him by another binds karma, a monk should not want it in his mind, nor ask for it or get it done by speech or body (13.1). The chapter goes through the feet, the whole body, wounds, and boils, piles and fistulas: wiping, pressing, oiling, rubbing with paste, washing, smearing, perfuming, cutting open and draining (13.2–34). Then sweat and dirt, ear and eye wax, trimming hair, picking out lice (13.35–38); being laid in a lap or on a cot, adorned with necklaces and a crown, taken to a garden to be served (13.39–41).
  9. Bear illness rather than harm beings (ch.13). If a householder offers to treat a sick monk with mantra power, pure or impure, or by digging up living roots and greens, the monk does not accept. Pain is sharp, the sutra says, but all living beings feel pain (13.42). The commentary adds that not seeking treatment is the ideal, but an ordinary monk living in a group may, when it cannot be avoided, accept blameless treatment; monks under special vows do not, even then (pp. 357–358).
  10. Nor from one another (ch.14, Anyonya-kriya-saptika). All of chapter 13 applies again between monk and monk, nun and nun: they must not wipe, press or tend each other’s bodies without cause (14.1–2). The commentary makes clear this forbids service done for comfort, pride, habit or fondness; serving a sick, old, fasting or newly ordained monk with a detached mind is still the duty of group-living monks (p. 361).

Key passages

8.5 · The fourth standing resolve

अहावरा चउत्था पडिमा– अचित्तं खलु उवसज्जेज्जा, णो अवलंबेज्जा, णो काएण विप्परिकम्मादी, णो सवियारं ठाणं ठाइस्सामि । वोसट्ठकाए वोसट्ठकेस-मंसु-लोम-णहे संणिरुद्धं वा ठाणं ठाइस्सामि त्ति चउत्था पडिमा ।
इच्चेयासिं चउण्हं पडिमाणं जाव पग्गहियतरागं विहारं विहरेज्जा, णेव किंचि वि वएज्जा ।

The fourth resolve: “I will take a lifeless place; I will not lean on anything, I will not stretch or bend my limbs, I will not step about. Having given up the body, and care of my hair, beard, body-hair and nails, I will stand there, fully restrained.” Having taken any one of these four resolves, the monk lives by it, and says nothing against others.

10.23 · How to relieve oneself

से भिक्खू वा भिक्खुणी वा सपाययं वा परपाययं वा गहाय से तमायाए एगंतमवक्कमेज्जा, अणावायंसि असंलोयंसि अप्पपाणंसि जाव मक्कडासंताणयंसि अहारामंसि वा उवस्सयंसि वा तओ संजयामेव उच्चारपासवणं वोसिरेज्जा, उच्चारपासवणं वोसिरित्ता से तमायाए एगंतमवक्कमेज्जा, अणावायंसि जाव मक्कडासंताणयंसि अहारामंसि वा झामथंडिलंसि वा अण्णयरंसि वा तहप्पगारंसि थंडिलंसि अचित्तंसि तओ संजयामेव उच्चार-पासवणं परिट्ठवेज्जा ।

A monk or nun takes his own pot or another’s and goes to a lonely spot in a garden or the lodging, where no one comes, no one sees, and there are no living beings or cobwebs, and there carefully relieves himself. Then he takes the pot to a lonely, lifeless place, such as ground outside a garden or burnt ground, and empties it there with care.

11.19 · No attachment to any sound

से भिक्खू वा भिक्खुणी वा इहलोइएहिं सद्देहिं, परलोइएहिं सद्देहिं, सुएहिं सद्देहिं, असुएहिं सद्देहिं, दिट्ठेहिं सद्देहिं, अदिट्ठेहिं सद्देहिं, इट्ठेहिं सद्देहिं, कंतेहिं सद्देहिं णो सज्जेज्जा, णो रज्जेज्जा, णो गिज्झेज्जा, णो मुज्जेज्जा, णो अज्झोववज्जेज्जा ।

A monk or nun should not cling to, delight in, crave, be infatuated by, or be lost in any sound: sounds of this world or of the other world, sounds heard or unheard, seen or unseen, wished-for or lovely.

13.1 · Service from another binds

परकिरियं अज्झत्थियं संसेइयं णो तं साइए, णो तं णियमे ।

Bodily service done to him by another binds karma. He should not want it in his mind, and should not ask for it or have it done by speech or body.

13.42 · No cure that harms beings

सिया से परो सुद्धेणं वा वइबलेणं तेइच्छं आउट्टे, सिया से परो असुद्धेणं वइबलेणं तेइच्छं आउट्टे, सिया से परो गिलाणस्स सचित्ताणि कंदाणि वा मूलाणि वा तयाणि वा हरियाणि वा खणित्तु वा कड्ढेत्तु कड्ढावेत्तु वा तेइच्छं आउट्टेज्जा, णो तं साइए णो तं णियमे ।
कडुवेयणा कट्टु वेयणा पाण-भूय-जीव-सत्ता वेयणं वेदेंति ।

If a householder wants to treat him with the power of a mantra, pure or impure, or to treat the sick monk by digging up or pulling out living roots, bulbs, bark or greens, he should not want it or have it done. Bitter is the pain, sharp is the pain; yet all breathing, living, sentient beings feel pain.

14.1 · Nor service between monks

से भिक्खू वा भिक्खुणी वा अण्णमण्णकिरियं अज्झत्थियं संसेइयं णो तं साइए णो तं णियमे ।

Mutual service between monks, or between nuns, also binds karma. A monk or nun should not want it in the mind, and should not ask for it or have it done by speech or body.

Note on 11.19: the print reads मुज्जेज्जा; the translation and the word-meanings read it as मुज्झेज्जा (“be infatuated”). The print is kept as printed.
