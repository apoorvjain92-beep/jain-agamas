Acharang 2.1 · Pindaishana (પિંડૈષણા): Seeking Alms

In one line: a monk or nun accepts only food and water that is free of life and free of faults, taken without greed, deceit or harm to anyone, and eats it with equanimity, because how the food is sought decides whether the restraint it feeds stays pure.

Source: Acharang Sutra, Book 2 (Second Shrutaskandha), chapter 1 (105 sutras, 11 sections), translated pages 71–163 (data/translations/acharang-2/). The book numbers the sutras afresh in each section, so references below read section.sutra (1.3.5 = section 3, sutra 5). Every section closes with the same refrain: “this is the whole of the monk’s or nun’s conduct; endowed with the careful practices (samiti), let them always strive.” 25 minutes.

What the chapter teaches

  1. Food must be both lifeless and faultless. The commentary names two tests: the food must be free of life (prasuka) and free of the faults of seeking (eshaniya). Food touched by insects, moss, seeds, green plants, raw water or living dust is refused; if taken by mistake, the living things are picked out in a secluded place, and what cannot be cleaned is laid down on lifeless ground. Whole or under-roasted grain that can still sprout is refused; split or well-roasted grain is accepted (1.1.1–3).
  2. No food made or bought for monks. Food cooked, bought, borrowed, snatched, taken without the owner’s leave or carried in for one monk or nun, or for many, is never accepted, even after others have eaten from it. Food made for “shramanas, brahmins, guests, the poor and beggars” in general may be taken only once it has passed to someone else, been taken out or been eaten from. The monk does not go for alms with followers of other faiths, householders or lax monks, avoids families famous for giving daily charity, and goes to ordinary, blameless families. If he hears devout people say “let us give them our own food and cook again for ourselves”, or sees a relative start cooking for him, he refuses, and he says so at once instead of keeping quiet and refusing later; nor does he visit his relatives’ homes before alms time (1.1.4–11, 1.2.2, 1.9.1–3).
  3. Stay away from festivals and feasts. At festival meals served to crowds of beggars the monk waits until the giving is done, then asks a family member. He does not even plan to go to a feast (sankhadi) more than half a yojana away; he turns the other way. The commentary lists the harms: craving for taste, food made specially for him, sickness from rich food, jostling crowds, drink and sexual danger, and the deceit of loitering near a feast house. Only an uncrowded feast, on a clear path, may be visited, as an exception (1.2.1–5, 1.3.1–4, 1.4.1).
  4. Doubt means no. If the monk is unsure whether food is acceptable, he does not take it. He carries his needed requisites, does not go out in rain, fog, dust storms or swarms of insects, and does not take food from the houses of crowned kings (1.3.5–8).
  5. Do not make the householder’s work harder. He does not enter while cows are being milked or food is still cooking; he waits out of sight. He does not hurry for the first portion set aside for charity, does not take a straight path that is broken, blocked by fierce animals or full of feeding birds, does not open a closed gate without permission, and does not stand where other beggars can see him and be crowded out (1.4.2, 1.5.1–6, 1.6.1).
  6. Dignity at the door. He does not lean on the doorframe, stand near the washing or bathing place, peer into the house, point with his finger, flatter the giver, or speak harshly when refused. If the giver washes a hand or ladle with raw water to serve him, or fans hot food, he asks them not to, and refuses if they do (1.6.2–7, 1.7.6).
  7. Food resting on living things is refused. Food fetched down by ladder from a height, dug out of a deep jar, taken from a clay-sealed pot, or placed on earth, water, fire, plants or creatures is refused, because the householder may fall or harm the six kinds of living beings. Raw, uncut vegetables, fruit, sprouts, garlic and seeds “untouched by a weapon” are refused; wash-water is accepted only when its taste and colour have changed (1.7.1–10, 1.8.1–16).
  8. No deceit about food. Sending visiting monks to other villages so as to eat first at one’s own relatives’ homes, eating the good portions and throwing away the plain, keeping a shared gift for oneself, giving choice pieces to favourites, hiding tasty food under plain food, or eating on the way back are all called “a station of deceit” (maya-sthana). Extra food is offered first to nearby fellow monks. Not relishing smells is also taught here (1.4.3, 1.5.7, 1.8.2, 1.9.4–7, 1.10.1–3).
  9. Honesty when things go wrong. If something unwanted, such as salt given in place of sugar, is accepted by mistake, the monk goes back and asks the giver whether it was given knowingly. Food meant for another person is taken only with that person’s leave. Food with much waste (bony fruit, cane pieces, shells) is refused (1.9.8, 1.10.4–7).
  10. Serve the sick truthfully, and take vows without pride. A monk carrying good food for a sick monk must not run it down as bitter or sour to keep it for himself. The chapter ends with the seven ways of seeking food and seven of seeking water, from “clean hand, clean vessel” to “food others throw away”, taken as personal vows; whoever takes one must not say “they are wrong, I alone am right” (1.11.1–11).

Key passages

1.1.1 · Food touched by living things

से भिक्खू वा भिक्खुणी वा गाहावइकुलं पिंडवायपडियाए अणुपविट्ठे समाणे से जं पुण जाणेज्जा– असणं वा पाणं वा खाइमं वा साइमं वा; पाणेहिं वा पणएहिं वा बीएहिं वा हरिएहिं वा; संसत्तं, उम्मिस्सं, सीओदएण वा ओसित्तं, रयसा वा परिघासियं; तहप्पगारं असणं वा पाणं वा खाइमं वा साइमं वा; परहत्थंसि वा परपायंसि वा अफासुयं अणेसणिज्जं ति मण्णमाणे लाभे वि संते णो पडिगाहेज्जा ।

When a monk or nun has entered a householder’s home for alms and sees that the food, drink, snacks or relishes are touched or mixed with small creatures, moss, seeds or green plants, sprinkled with raw water or covered with living dust, then, whether it is in the giver’s hand or vessel, knowing it to be living and unacceptable, they should not accept it even when it is offered.

1.3.5 · When in doubt, do not take

से भिक्खू वा भिक्खुणी वा गाहावइकुलं पिंडवायपडियाए अणुपविट्ठे समाणे से जं पुण जाणेज्जा– असणं वा पाणं वा खाइमं वा साइमं वा एसणिज्जे सिया, अणेसणिज्जे सिया ? वितिगिंछासमावण्णेण अप्पाणेणं असमाहडाए लेस्साए तहप्पगारं असणं वा पाणं वा खाइमं वा साइमं वा लाभे संते णो पडिगाहेज्जा।

If a monk or nun, in a householder’s home, wonders, “Is this food acceptable, or is it not?”, then with the mind in doubt and unsettled they should not accept that food, even when it is offered.

1.9.5 · Eat the good with the plain

से भिक्खू वा भिक्खुणी वा गाहावईकुलं पिंडवाय पडियाए पविट्ठे समाणे अण्णयरं भोयणजायं पडिगाहेत्ता सुब्भिं-सुब्भि भोच्चा दुब्भिं-दुब्भि परिट्ठवेइ। माइट्ठाणं संफासे । णो एवं करेज्जा । सुब्भि वा दुब्भि वा सव्वं भुंजे ण छड्डए ।

If a monk or nun accepts some food, eats the tasty parts and throws away the poor parts, that is deceit. They should not do this. Good or poor, they should eat all of it and throw nothing away.

1.10.2 · Hide nothing in the bowl

से एगइओ मणुण्णं भोयणजायं पडिगाहेत्ता पंतेण भोयणेण पलिच्छाएइ मामेयं दाइयं संतं; दट्टूणं सयमाइए; तं जहा– आयरिए वा जाव गणावच्छेइए वा । णो खलु मे कस्सइ किंचि वि दायव्वं सिया । माइट्ठाणं संफासे । णो एवं करेज्जा ।
से तमायाए तत्थ गच्छेज्जा, गच्छेत्ता पुव्वामेव उत्ताणए हत्थे पडिग्गहं कट्टु इमं खलु इमं खलु त्ति आलोएज्जा । णो किंचि वि णिगूहेज्जा ।

A monk receives fine food and covers it with plain food, thinking, “If the acharya or the other elders see it, they will take it for themselves; I should not have to give any of it to anyone.” That is deceit; he should not do it. He should take the food to them, hold the bowl up on his open hand and show it: “This is here, and this is here.” He should hide nothing at all.

1.11.11 · No pride in one’s vow

इच्चेयासिं सत्तण्हं पिंडेसणाणं सत्तण्हं पाणेसणाणं अण्णयरं पडिमं पडिवज्जमाणे णो एवं वएज्जा– मिच्छा पडिवण्णा खलु एए भयंतारो, अहमेगे सम्मं पडिवण्णे ।
जे एए भयंतारो एयाओ पडिमाओ पडिवज्जित्ताणं विहरंति, जो य अहमंसि एयं पडिमं पडिवज्जित्ताणं विहरामि; सव्वे ते उ जिणाणाए उवट्ठिया अण्णोण्णसमाहीए; एवं च णं विहरंति ।

Whoever takes up any one of these seven ways of seeking food or seven ways of seeking water should not say, “These revered monks have taken it up wrongly; I alone have taken it up rightly.” Rather: “Those revered monks who live by these vows, and I who live by this one, are all standing in the Jina’s command, each according to his own peace of mind.” This is how they live.
