Uttaradhyayan Sutra: All 36 Chapters

The Uttaradhyayan holds Bhagwan Mahavira’s last teachings: 36 chapters on how to live, what is real, and how the soul gets free. Each module below gives the chapter’s point in one line, what it teaches in the chapter’s own order, and its key verses in Prakrit with plain English, checked against the scans.

New here? Start with 3 (the four rare things), 10 (the falling leaf), 9 (Nami), 25 (the true brahmin), 28 (the path) and 32 (sources of negligence).

#ChapterIn one line
1Humility
Vinayashruta		humility (vinaya) towards the teacher is the root of dharma: the humble disciple who obeys, restrains himself and welcomes correction gains knowledge, honour, and in the end liberation or a high heaven.
2Enduring Hardships
Parishaha		a monk will meet twenty-two kinds of hardship, from hunger and insults to doubt about the faith, and he bears each one calmly, inside his vows, as a way of shedding karma.
3The Four Rare Things
Chaturangiya		four things are hard to get, each harder than the one before: a human life, hearing the dharma, believing it, and actually living it. The one who gets all four ends suffering for good.
4Life Cannot Be Mended
Asamskrita		a broken life cannot be joined again, and neither wealth nor family can save you from your own karma, so do not put dharma off: live awake, without negligence (pramada), until the body ends.
5Two Kinds of Death
Akamamaraniya		everyone dies, but the fool dies unwillingly, in fear, again and again, while the one who lives with restraint meets death calmly, by choice, and so cuts the round of births short.
6The Monk Free of Knots
Kshullaka-Nirgranthiya		ignorance ties us in knots, and neither family, wealth nor clever words can untie them; only a monk who drops every possession and attachment, and stays watchful, becomes truly free of knots (nirgrantha).
7The Fattened Ram
Urabhriya		the one who fattens on pleasure is like a ram fed well only for the guest’s table: he trades a long, bright future for a few short years of enjoyment, so keep your human birth as capital and grow it.
8Kapila and Greed
Kapiliya		to escape a bad rebirth, let go of attachments, harm no living being, eat only to keep up restraint, and see through greed, which grows with every gain, as Kapila found when two grams of gold turned into a wish for millions.
9Nami's Renunciation
Namipravrajya (नमिप्रव्रज्या)		when King Nami leaves his kingdom, Indra comes disguised as a Brahmin and tests him with ten worldly duties, and Nami answers each one by turning it inward: the real city, fort, war and wealth are inside the soul.
10The Falling Leaf
Drumapatraka		life falls like a yellow leaf from a tree, and a human birth with everything needed for dharma is very hard to get, so do not be careless (pramada) even for a moment.
11The Glory of the Learned
Bahushrut Puja (બહુશ્રુત પૂજા)		a monk becomes deeply learned (bahushrut) only through humility, self-control and staying with his teacher; such a learned monk shines above all others, and his learning carries him and others to liberation.
12The True Sacrifice
Harikeshiya (હરિકેશીય)		a monk born among outcastes shows the proud Brahmins at a sacrifice that a person is made high by conduct, not by birth, and that the true sacrifice and the true bath happen inside the soul, through austerity, restraint and dharma.
13Two Brothers, Two Paths
Chitta-Sambhutiya		two brothers who were together for five lives meet again in the sixth, one a monk and one an emperor; the monk urges the emperor to let go of pleasures, the emperor cannot, and so one reaches liberation and the other the lowest hell.
14King Ishukara
Ishukariya		two boys wake to their past life and leave home, and their reasons, that the soul is real, life is short and only dharma protects, win over their father, their mother, the queen and at last the king, until all six reach liberation.
15The True Monk
Sabhikshuka		begging does not make a monk. A true monk (bhikshu) lives for liberation alone: he wants no honour, bears every hardship calmly, earns nothing by magic or medicine, flatters no one, fears nothing, and wanders free and alone. Each verse ends with the same words, “he is a monk”.
16The Supports of Celibacy
Brahmacharya Samadhisthan		celibacy is the heart of a monk’s practice, and ten simple guards (where he lives, what he says, sees, hears, remembers, eats and wears, and how he treats the senses) keep it safe; the one who keeps it is honoured even by the gods and reaches liberation.
17The Bad Monk
Papashramaniya		a monk who takes his vows “like a lion” but then lives “like a jackal”, for comfort, sleep, tasty food and his own way, is called a bad monk (papa-shramana); the one who drops all these faults is honoured like nectar.
18King Sanjaya
Sanjayiya (Samyatiya)		a hunting king meets a silent monk, learns that he too must give others the fearlessness he begged for, and renounces; a fellow monk then shows him that the great kings of old all won liberation by leaving one-sided views behind and walking the Jina’s path of knowledge and conduct.
19Mrigaputra
Mrigaputriya		a pampered prince sees a monk, remembers that he was once a monk himself, and answers every warning his parents give about the hard monk’s life with the far worse pains he has already suffered in hell, until they let him go to live “like a deer” and he reaches liberation.
20The Great Nirgrantha
Mahanirgranthiya		no wealth, family or doctor can protect us from suffering; only the soul that takes up restraint and keeps it truly has a protector, and a monk who lets his restraint slip becomes unprotected again.
21Samudrapala
Samudrapaliya		a rich young merchant sees a condemned man led to execution, understands that every bad deed bears its fruit, becomes a monk, bears every hardship with an even mind, and crosses the ocean of birth and death.
22Rathanemi
Rathanemiya		Prince Arishtanemi turns back from his own wedding out of compassion for the animals penned up for the feast; Rajemati follows him into renunciation, and later, alone in a cave, her firm words bring the wavering monk Rathanemi back to restraint.
23Keshi and Gautama
Keshi-Gautamiya		two great monks, one from Parshvanatha’s line and one from Mahavira’s, meet to settle why their rules differ; through twelve questions they show that the goal is one, the outer rules suit different people, and the real path is inner: conquering the mind, cutting craving, cooling the passions.
24Mothers of the Teaching
Pravachanamata		the whole of a monk’s life rests on eight “mothers”: five kinds of careful action (samiti) in walking, speaking, seeking alms, handling things and disposing of waste, and three restraints (gupti) of mind, speech and body; whoever keeps them well is soon free.
25The True Brahmin
Yajniya		a monk turned away from his brother’s sacrificial hall answers with a teaching, not anger: a true brahmin is made by inner qualities and deeds, not by birth or ritual, and the real sacrifice is restraint; the brother is won over and both reach liberation.
26Proper Conduct
Samachari		a monk’s whole day and night, from the ten courtesies he shows his teacher and fellow monks to the hours of study, meditation, alms, sleep and confession, is laid out as one careful routine, and living it frees him from all suffering.
27The Unruly Bullocks
Khalunkiya		a good disciple carries the cart of dharma like a willing ox, but bad disciples behave like unruly bullocks, and the elder Garga, unable to set his own disciples right, left them and went on alone, calm and steady.
28The Path to Liberation
Mokshamarga Gati		the path to liberation has four parts: knowledge, faith, conduct and austerity. Knowledge shows what is true, faith holds to it, conduct stops new karma coming in, and austerity burns up the old.
29Right Effort
Samyak Parakram (Sammatta-parakkame)		for each of 73 practices, from longing for liberation to the final stillness, the disciple asks “Lord, what does the soul gain by this?”, and each answer shows how that one practice, done rightly, carries the soul all the way to freedom.
30The Path of Austerity
Tapomarga Gati		first close the channels through which karma flows in. Then dry up what has gathered over countless lives with twelve kinds of austerity: six outer, which work through the body, and six inner, which work through the mind and heart.
31Rules of Conduct
Charan Vidhi		conduct comes down to one rule: turn away from lack of restraint and turn toward restraint. The chapter then counts up from one to thirty-three, naming what a monk must give up, take up or simply know. Each verse ends with the same promise: “that monk does not stay in the round of rebirth.”
32Sources of Negligence
Pramadasthana		suffering does not come from the objects of the senses but from our attachment and aversion toward them; the one who stays even-minded among them, like a lotus leaf in water, is freed from all suffering.
33The Nature of Karma
Karma Prakriti		the soul wanders because it is bound by eight kinds of karma; know their kinds, their length and their strength, and then stop new karma coming in and destroy the old.
34The Colours of the Soul
Leshya		the soul’s states, coloured by the passions, come in six shades from black to white; the dark three lead to a bad birth and the bright three to a good one, so a monk should leave the dark and hold to the bright.
35The Homeless Monk's Path
Anagara Marga Gati		a monk who has left home must not let home’s bonds creep back; he lives in a simple place, does not build, cook, buy or sell, eats only to keep going, wants no honour, and so ends all suffering.
36The Living and the Non-living
Jivajiva-Vibhakti		the whole world is made of the living and the non-living; the chapter maps every kind of each, from atoms to the siddhas, so that a monk who knows and trusts this map delights in restraint, dies well, and reaches liberation.
