Lesson 34 · The Careful Monk: Alms, Lodging, Walking, Speech

Unit 6: The first Anga and the daily essentials (Acharang, Avashyak) · about 70 minutes

Aim: You will see how Book 2 of the Acharang turns the non-violence of Book 1 into everyday detail (how a monk seeks food, finds a place to stay, walks the road and speaks) and you will be able to apply its rules of speech to your own talk.

Read

  • Acharang 2.1: Seeking Alms: look for the two tests every piece of food must pass, the rule “doubt means no”, and the many “stations of deceit”.
  • Acharang 2.2: Seeking Lodging and Acharang 2.3: Careful Walking: notice that a place to stay and a road to walk are judged by the same care as food, and how calm the monk stays when he is thrown into a river or robbed.
  • Acharang 2.4: Kinds of Speech: look for the four kinds of speech, and why even true words can be sinful.

Key ideas

  1. Food is taken only if it is lifeless, faultless and given without burden. Food must be free of life (prasuka) and free of the faults of seeking (eshaniya): anything touched by insects, seeds, green plants, raw water or living dust is refused, and so is food cooked, bought or carried in for the monk (Ach 2.1 s.1.1–11). If he is unsure whether food is acceptable, he does not take it (Ach 2.1 s.3.5). He must not make the householder’s work harder, stand where other beggars are crowded out, or flatter the giver; and eating the tasty parts while throwing away the plain, or hiding fine food under plain food in the bowl, is called “a station of deceit” (maya-sthana): “Good or poor, they should eat all of it” (Ach 2.1 s.4.2–6.7, s.9.5, s.10.2).

  2. A place to stay and a road to walk are judged by the same care. A lodging full of eggs, insects or cobwebs is not used; a clear one is used only after inspecting and sweeping it, and never one built or repaired for monks (Ach 2.2 s.1.1–8). The monk asks the owner’s permission “for as long as you allow and in as much space as you give”, and lives in any lodging, even or uneven, dusty or full of mosquitoes, “without the slightest dejection” (Ach 2.2 s.3.3, s.3.30). In the rains he stays in one place, because the paths are full of new life; on the road he looks a yoke’s length ahead and steps around small creatures (Ach 2.3 s.1.1, s.1.6). Asked by travellers which way an animal went, he stays silent or says “I do not know”, since the questioner may be a hunter (Ach 2.3 s.3.7). Thrown from a boat or robbed of his bowl, he does not rise to hurt anyone and complains to no village or king (Ach 2.3 s.2.3, s.3.15).

  3. Speech is weighed before it leaves the mouth. There are four kinds of speech: true, false, mixed, and ordinary everyday speech that is neither true nor false; a monk uses only well-weighed truth and ordinary speech, and only when they harm no one (Ach 2.4 s.1.3–6). Even a true word is not said if it is harsh, cutting or hurtful: he does not call anyone “leper” or “one-handed”, does not predict with certainty what will happen, does not wish for rain or for a king’s victory, and does not tell a woodcutter which tree is “fit for a door” (Ach 2.4 s.1.1, s.1.11–12, s.2.1, s.2.7–16). The whole chapter closes in one sutra:

    से भिक्खू वा भिक्खुणी वा वंता कोहं च माणं च मायं च लोभं च अणुवीइ णिट्ठाभासी णिसम्मभासी अतुरियभासी विवेगभासी समियाए संजए भासं भासेज्जा ।

    The monk or nun, having cast out anger, pride, deceit and greed, should speak as one restrained and careful in speech: after reflecting, with certainty, after hearing and understanding, without haste, and with discernment.

    Source: Acharang 2.4 s.2.19 (Bhashajata) · checked against the scan, as given in the module

Connect

Book 1 of the Acharang (Lessons 30–33) taught that every one of the six kinds of life feels pain; Book 2 shows what that looks like at a householder’s door, in a borrowed room and on a muddy road. These four chapters are three of the five careful practices you met as the “eight mothers” in Lesson 17 (careful walking, careful speech, careful seeking): see Samiti and Gupti. The alms rules and the bee-like taking of Lessons 24 and 26 are the same teaching (Bhramar Vritti, Ahimsa), and the module notes that the speech chapter is very close to Dashvaikalik 7 from Lesson 27. Speech “moved by anger, pride, deceit or greed” is the four passions of Kashaya at work in the mouth.

Reflect

  1. When you are unsure whether something is right (a deal, a remark, a purchase) do you go ahead or hold back? What would “doubt means no” change for you?
  2. Do you ever “hide the fine food under the plain”, keeping the best of something for yourself while looking fair to others?
  3. Which of your true remarks this month hurt someone more than they helped?

Practice this week

The speech rules are monks’ rules, but they need no robe. A householder’s version: for one week, before you say anything sharp, pause and check it against Ach 2.4 s.2.19: is it said from anger, pride, deceit or greed? Have I heard the other person fully? Am I hurrying? Also drop two habits the chapter names: calling people by a defect or insult (even in jest), and saying with certainty what will or will not happen (Ach 2.4 s.1.1, s.2.1). Each evening, note one sentence you changed or held back.

Self-check

Question

  1. What two tests must every piece of food pass before a monk accepts it?

It must be free of life (prasuka) and free of the faults of seeking (eshaniya) (Ach 2.1 s.1.1–3).

Question

  1. What does a monk do if he is unsure whether some food is acceptable?

He does not take it, even when it is offered (Ach 2.1 s.3.5).

Question

  1. Travellers ask a monk whether he saw an animal pass. What does he do, and why?

He stays silent, or says “I do not know”, because the questioner may be a hunter and he would share in the killing (Ach 2.3 s.3.7).

Question

  1. Of the four kinds of speech, which may a monk use?

Subtle, well-weighed true speech and ordinary everyday speech, and only when they are free of sin and harm no being (Ach 2.4 s.1.3–6).
