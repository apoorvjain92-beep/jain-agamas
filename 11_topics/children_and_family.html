Topic · Raising Children and Family Life

What the Agamas in this vault say about raising children and family life. From the Uttaradhyayan, Dashvaikalik, Acharang and Avashyak.

To be honest up front: these four books say almost nothing directly about how to raise a child. They are books for monks and nuns, and no verse here tells a parent how to feed, teach or discipline a son or daughter. What they do have is of three kinds. First, stories in which parents and children meet at the turning point of a life, nearly always when a son wants to leave home to become a monk. Second, a long and detailed teaching on how a teacher (guru) trains a pupil, which is the books’ real picture of raising someone. Third, a steady teaching on family love and its limits, plus a few householder rules in the Avashyak that touch the people in one’s care. Much of the most useful detail is in the commentary (vivechan), not in the sutra itself, and this page says which is which.

Parents and children in the stories

  • Parents in these stories love their children and try to hold on to them. Mrigaputra is “dear to his parents”; when he asks to become a monk, they spend twenty verses telling him the monk’s life is too hard for someone so delicate, and urge him to enjoy pleasures first and practise dharma in old age (Utt 19.2, 19.25–44, Utt 19). The priest Bhrigu argues that men without sons reach no good state, offers his sons wealth, and says he is like a tree without branches, a bird without wings, without them (Utt 14.8–11, 14.29, Utt 14).
  • The children answer with reasons, and the parents listen. Bhrigu’s sons answer each of his points: the Vedas do not protect, and “sons who are born do not protect either” (Utt 14.12). Mrigaputra answers his parents’ warnings with what he has already suffered in past lives (Utt 19.45–75). In both stories the parents are won over: Mrigaputra’s say “go, son, do as brings you happiness” (Utt 19.85–86), and Bhrigu and his wife Yasha follow their sons into renunciation (Utt 14.29–36).
  • A parent’s fear can shape a child, but not decide the child’s path. The commentary on Utt 14 tells that Bhrigu and Yasha, warned that their sons would become monks, taught the boys to fear monks and keep away from them. The boys, hiding in a tree, watched a monk eat his simple food with care, and remembered their past life anyway (Utt 14.1–5 and commentary, translated p. 329).
  • A child’s nature is not simply the parents’ doing. In the commentary to Utt 12, the boy Harikeshbal was born ugly and grew angry and quarrelsome because of pride in a past life; “even his parents grew weary” of his temper. What changed him was not punishment but seeing, for himself, that beings suffer for their faults and are loved for their virtues (Utt 12, commentary pp. 284–286). Utt 13 makes the same point with two brothers: together for five lives, apart in the sixth because of one brother’s own wish (Utt 13.6–8, Utt 13).
  • Families also decide marriages, and some wake up together. In Utt 22 the elders arrange Arishtanemi’s marriage to Rajemati, and her father sets the terms (Utt 22.6–8); after both renounce, Rajemati as a nun brings many of her kin into renunciation (Utt 22.32, Utt 22). In Utt 14 all six members of two households reach liberation one after another (Utt 14.49–53).

सो बिंतऽम्मा पियरो, एवमेयं जहा फुडं ।
इह लोए णिप्पिवासस्स, णत्थि किंचि वि दुक्करं ॥४५॥

He said, “Mother and father, it is just as you say, the monk’s life is hard. But for one who has no thirst for the things of this world, nothing at all is hard.”

Source: Utt 19.45 · Uttaradhyayan 19 · scan

How a teacher trains a pupil

  • The good pupil needs only a sign; the bad one needs the whip. A bad horse keeps needing the whip, while a good horse turns from the wrong path at the mere sight of it (Utt 1.12). A teacher enjoys teaching a willing pupil as a rider enjoys a good horse, and suffers with a foolish one (Utt 1.37, Utt 1).
  • The same correction is heard two ways. The pupil with a wrong outlook feels the teacher’s correction as pushes, slaps and abuse, and feels like a slave; the good pupil feels treated like a son, a brother or a kinsman (Utt 1.38–39). The wise take even harsh instruction as being for their good (Utt 1.29).
  • The teacher also has a duty, and can be wrong. The guru raises the pupil to a high place “as a father settles his daughter in a good family”; the commentary says this verse shows the duties of both, guru and disciple (Dash 9.3.13 and commentary, Dash 9). The Acharang compares teachers caring for new monks “day and night” to parent birds caring for their young until their wings grow (Ach 1.6.3 s.5, Ach 1.6). In the commentary to Utt 1.13, the teacher Chandrarudra strikes his new disciple’s head in anger; the disciple bears it calmly, and the teacher afterwards asks the disciple’s forgiveness.
  • Some pupils will not be set right. The elder Garga’s disciples behave like unruly bullocks: they sulk, make excuses and answer back. “I taught them, initiated them and fed them, yet like swans whose wings have grown they fly off in every direction.” Garga leaves them and goes on alone, gentle and composed (Utt 27.8–17, Utt 27).
  • Beating appears in these books as a fact of that time, not as advice. Dash 9.2.13–15 notes that delicate princes put up with being bound and beaten by the teachers who taught them worldly crafts, and still honoured those teachers. The verse uses this to show how much more a monk should honour his own teacher. It describes old schooling; it does not tell anyone to beat a child.

हियं विगयभया बुद्धा, फरुसं पि अणुसासणं ।
वेस्सं तं होइ मूढाणं, खंति-सोहिकरं पयं ॥२९॥

The fearless and wise take even harsh instruction as being for their good. To fools, those same words, which bring forgiveness and purity, become a cause of hatred.

Source: Utt 1.29 · Uttaradhyayan 1 · scan

Respect for parents, teachers and elders

  • Humility (vinaya) is the root of dharma. As a tree grows from its root to fruit, dharma grows from humility, and liberation is its highest fruit (Dash 9.2.1–2, Dash 9). The humble one carries out instructions, stays near, and reads the elder’s signs and moods (Utt 1.2). The learning file draws the same lesson for parents and elders (Relationships §6).
  • Respect shows in small daily things. Rise when called, ask with joined hands, sit lower, do not interrupt; if the elder is angry, calm him with folded hands and say “I will not do it again” (Utt 1.17–23, 1.41; Dash 9.2.17–20).
  • Scorn no one, young or old. One is worthy of honour who does not look down on anyone, “whether child or elderly person, woman or man”, monk or householder (Dash 9.3.12). Nor should a pupil look down on a teacher as “young” (Dash 9.1.2–3).
  • A mother teaches her son to let go of anger. In the commentary to Utt 1.14, a nobleman’s son catches his brother’s killer; his mother tells him his greatness lies in calming his anger, and he lets the man go (Utt 1).
  • Parents’ consent matters. Every renouncing son in these stories asks his parents’ leave (Utt 14.7, 19.10–11, 19.85–86). The commentary on Dash 6.14 states it as a rule: a monk may not initiate anyone without the permission of their parents; doing so is theft of a living being (translated p. 313, Dash 6).

Family love, and what it cannot do

  • Family cannot protect you, and you cannot protect them. The Acharang says the relatives one lives with may turn against one in old age or illness, or one against them; neither side can be the other’s shelter (Ach 1.2.1 s.2, Ach 1.2). “Mother, father, daughter-in-law, brother, wife and your own sons” cannot protect you from your own karma (Utt 6.3, Utt 6), and no relative can share your suffering (Utt 13.23).
  • “Mine” is the chain. The person who craves sense objects says “my mother, my father, my brother, my sister, my wife, my sons, my daughters”, and so works day and night and harms living beings again and again (Ach 1.2.1 s.1). People also do harm “for the family” (Ach 1.2.2 s.4–5).
  • Love does not stop pain. In Utt 20 a rich man’s son lies in burning pain: his father would give all his wealth, his mother, brothers and sisters try, his wife never leaves his side, and none can free him (Utt 20.18–30, Utt 20).
  • Family tears are a test, not a wrong. When one sets out to renounce, parents weep, “Do not leave us; we shall do as you wish”; the seeker does not take refuge in them (Ach 1.6.1 s.8). The commentary says that after some time the parents’ attachment settles.
  • The modern note at the back of the Acharang draws the line between love and infatuation. Muni Santbalji’s reflections, printed as Appendix 1 of the Acharang volume (not sutra or commentary), say these passages do not ask anyone to ignore the real affection of mother, father or family: what is to be given up is infatuation (moha), not duty. A householder should take craving out of family ties and keep a relationship of duty (Acharang Part 1, appendix, pp. 465–466).

जेहिं वा सद्धिं संवसइ ते वा णं एगया णियगा तं पुव्विं परिवयंति, सो वा ते णियगे पच्छा परिवएज्जा । णालं ते तव ताणाए वा सरणाए वा, तुमं पि तेसिं णालं ताणाए वा सरणाए वा । से ण हासाए, ण किड्डाए, ण रईए, ण विभूसाए ।

The very relatives he lives with may one day speak against him first, or he may later speak against them. They are not able to protect you or give you shelter, and you are not able to protect them or give them shelter. (In old age) he is no longer fit for laughter, play, love or adornment.

Source: Ach 1.2.1 s.2 · Acharang 1.2 · scan

Mahavira as a son

  • His parents named him for a quality. Because the family’s wealth grew from the day he was conceived, his parents named him Vardhamana (Ach 2.15 s.8, 11, Ach 2.15). The commentary notes that families of that time gave a child a name that fitted some quality they saw in him.
  • He grew up in care. He was raised by five nurses (for milk, bathing, dressing, play and carrying), “moved from one lap to another”, and grew “like a champaka tree sheltered in a mountain cave” (Ach 2.15 s.9).
  • His parents were practising lay followers. They followed Parshva’s tradition, kept the lay vows for many years, and at the end confessed, repented and died by the final fast (sallekhana) (Ach 2.15 s.13).
  • He waited for his parents. The sutra says only that after thirty years at home, his parents gone, “his vow fulfilled”, he renounced (Ach 2.15 s.14). The commentary says the sutra does not state the vow, and gives the tradition: in the womb he lay still so as not to hurt his mother, she feared she had lost the child, and seeing her grief he vowed not to renounce while his parents lived (translated p. 376). The commentary to Ach 1.9 adds that he stayed two more years at his elder brother Nandivardhana’s request (Ach 1.9.1 s.11, Ach 1.9).
  • He had a family of his own. The sutra names his wife Yashoda, his daughter (Anojja or Priyadarshana) and his granddaughter, but says nothing more about them (Ach 2.15 s.12).

The householder and those in his care

  • The lay vows guard dependants from cruelty. The five faults of the first lay vow are tying up, beating, cutting, overloading, and withholding food and water (Av 7 s.2, Av 7). The commentary lists children among those who must not be tied up in a way that makes them suffer, and says these faults come from passion or lack of discernment.
  • Purpose and passion make the difference. The commentary calls harm done for a real purpose and without passion “relative” harm, permitted to a householder; its example is giving a child bitter medicine the child dislikes (Av 7 s.2, translated p. 237).
  • No lies about a bride or groom. The first of the five gross lies a layman gives up is a lie about a bride or groom (Av 7 s.3). The commentary on the fourth vow says a householder may have to take part in arranging the marriages of his own sons and daughters, but should not busy himself arranging other people’s (Av 7 s.5, translated p. 244).
  • Possessions include people in one’s keeping. The limit on possessions covers “two-footed and four-footed beings” too, and possession is really attachment (Av 7 s.6).

थूलगपाणाइवायं समणोवासओ पच्चक्खाइ, से पाणाइवाए दुविहे पण्णत्ते, तं जहा- संकप्पओ अ आरंभओ अ । तत्थ समणोवासओ संकप्पओ जावज्जीवाए पच्चक्खाइ, नो आरंभओ । थूलगपाणाइवायवेरमणस्स समणोवासएणं इमे पंच अइयारा जाणियव्वा, (ण समायरियव्वा) तं जहा- बंधे, वहे, छविच्छेए, अइभारे, भत्तपाणवुच्छेए ।

The lay follower gives up gross violence to living beings. That violence is of two kinds: intended and arising from work. Of these he gives up intended violence for life, not violence arising from work. He should know, but not commit, five faults of this vow: tying up, beating, cutting the skin, overloading, and withholding food and water.

Source: Av 7 s.2 · Avashyak 7 · scan

Applying it

Everything in this section is my application for a parent, drawn from the themes above. None of it is the books’ own words.

  • Raise the “good horse”, not the whip. The books praise the pupil who turns at a sign and see constant force as a sign that something has failed (Utt 1.12, 27.3). For a parent: aim for a child who listens to a look or a word, and treat needing to shout or strike as your problem to solve, not a method. The Avashyak’s faults (tying, beating, withholding food) and the commentary’s test of “passion or purpose” give a simple check: was this for the child’s good, or my anger? (Av 7 s.2)
  • Correct like a father settling a daughter well, and apologise when you are wrong. The good pupil hears correction as love when it comes from someone who treats him “like a son” (Utt 1.39, Dash 9.3.13). For a parent: let correction come with visible care, and, like Chandrarudra, ask your child’s forgiveness when you correct in anger (Utt 1.13 commentary).
  • Teach respect by small habits, including respect for the young. Rising when called, speaking gently, saying “I will not do it again” are the books’ marks of humility (Utt 1.41). For a parent: teach these at home, and model the other half too: scorn no one, “child or elderly person” (Dash 9.3.12).
  • Hold them, but do not own them. The books’ sternest family teaching is that “mine” is a chain and no one can carry another’s karma (Ach 1.2.1 s.1–2, Utt 13.23). For a parent: love and protect, but remember the child is a soul on its own path, as Bhrigu’s sons were despite their parents’ fear of monks (Utt 14 commentary). Keep the tie one of duty and care rather than possession (Santbalji’s note, Ach appendix).
  • Let dharma be something the family does together. Mahavira’s parents were practising lay followers, and in Utt 14 a whole family woke together. For a parent: practise samayik, pratikraman or a day of fasting where the children can see it, and talk about why (Ach 2.15 s.13, Av 7 s.10).
  • Do not put dharma off until the children are grown. Bhrigu and Mrigaputra’s parents both said “practise dharma in old age”; the sons answered that only one who knows he will not die can say “tomorrow” (Utt 14.26–27, 19.44). For a parent: busy family years are not a reason to wait.
