Topic · Controlling the Ego (Pride)

What the Agamas in this vault say about pride and how to bring it down. From the Uttaradhyayan, Dashvaikalik, Acharang and Avashyak.

The books say a lot about pride (mana), more than about most everyday subjects. They name its kinds, show what it costs, give its cure, and build the daily rites around breaking it. But nearly all of it is spoken to a monk: pride before his guru, pride in his learning or his austerity, pride when someone bows to him. What follows gives what the texts actually say, with references. Where this page draws a lesson for a householder, it says so under Applying it. For the four passions as a whole see Kashaya, and for humility as a discipline see Vinaya. This page looks at one practical question: how do you keep pride under control?

What pride is, and what people are proud of

  • Pride is one of the four passions. Anger, pride, deceit and greed “increase sin”; the one who wants his own good should vomit out all four (Dash 8.37). The commentary on Utt 29 defines pride as the state of conceit and ego that arises when pride-deluding karma comes into effect (Utt 29 s.70, commentary). See Dash 8.
  • There are eight kinds (mada). The Uttaradhyayan’s list of what a monk must drop includes “the eight kinds of pride” (Utt 31.10). The Avashyak’s daily Pratikraman turns back from them in one line, atthahim mayathanehim (“the eight grounds of pride”), and the commentary names them: pride in caste (jati), family (kula), strength (bala), looks (rupa), austerity (tapa), learning (shruta), gain (labha) and power or authority (aishvarya) (Av 4 L8, pp. 148–149). See Utt 31 and Av 4.
  • Each kind comes with a cautionary example in the commentary. Harikeshi’s pride in caste led to birth among outcastes; Mahavira’s soul, as Marichi, was proud of his family; Duryodhana was proud of his strength, Sanatkumara of his beauty, Ravana of his power. The commentary’s rule is that whatever you are proud of, you do not get again (Av 4 L8, commentary, pp. 148–149). These are the commentary’s stories, not the sutra’s words.
  • The good things are the danger too. Pride in learning, austerity and intelligence is named alongside pride in birth (Dash 8.30). Even humility can become a cause of pride: one of the four kinds of composure is to be humble “and not become proud of being humble” (Dash 9.4). Utt 31 also lists “three prides” in special powers, tasty food and comfort (Utt 31.4).

ण जाइमत्ते ण य रूवमत्ते, ण लाभमत्ते ण सुएण मत्ते ।
मयाणि सव्वाणि विवज्जइत्ता, धम्मज्झाणरए जे स भिक्खू ॥

He is not proud of his birth, not proud of his looks, not proud of his gains, not proud of his learning. Giving up every kind of pride, he delights in meditation on dharma. He is a monk.

Source: Dash 10.19 · module (checked against the scan). The commentary notes that four kinds are named here and “all prides” covers the rest of the eight.

What pride costs

  • Pride destroys humility. Anger destroys love, pride destroys humility, deceit friendship, and greed everything (Dash 8.38). Unchecked, the passions “water the roots of rebirth” (Dash 8.40).
  • The proud cannot learn. Pride heads the five things that block learning: pride, anger, carelessness, illness and laziness. No one teaches a proud person, because he shows no respect (Utt 11.3). See Utt 11.
  • Pride ruins the proud, like the bamboo’s fruit. A monk who from stiff pride, anger, conceit or laziness will not learn humility before his guru destroys his own virtues, as the bamboo’s fruit kills the bamboo (Dash 9.1.1). See Dash 9.
  • Pride brings you down. Nami tells Indra: anger takes you down, pride lowers you (Utt 9.54). The bad monk is “stiff with pride” and speaks ill of the teachers who taught him (Utt 17.4–5, 17.11). See Utt 9 and Utt 17.
  • It binds low-status karma. The commentary on Av 4 says pride binds the karma of a low birth (nicha gotra) (Av 4 L8, commentary, p. 148). The commentary on Dash 10.18 says the same of praising oneself and slandering others (Dash 10.18, commentary).

The cure: gentleness (mardava)

  • Conquer pride with gentleness. Kill anger with calm, conquer pride with gentleness, deceit with straightforwardness, greed with contentment (Dash 8.39; the verse is given in full on Kashaya).
  • Gentleness pulls out all eight roots. Through gentleness the soul gains freedom from arrogance; mild and gentle by nature, it destroys the eight grounds of pride (Utt 29 s.51). See Utt 29.
  • Conquering pride does not only stop it. It undoes it. The commentary gives the rule “whatever passion one experiences, that one is bound”: while pride is active, pride-karma is bound. Turned into softness, it stops binding and old pride-karma is shed (Utt 29 s.69–70, commentary).
  • Conquer the one, conquer the rest. The senses and the four passions are hard to conquer, but when the self is conquered all of them are (Utt 9.36). The Acharang says the same: whoever sees anger sees pride, and overcoming one passion you overcome the others (Ach 1.3.4). See Ach 1.3.

माण-विजएणं भंते ! जीवे किं जणयइ ?
माण-विजएणं मद्दवं जणयइ, माणवेयणिज्जं कम्मं ण बंधइ, पुव्वबद्धं च णिज्जरेइ।

Lord, what does the soul gain by conquering pride? By conquering pride it gains softness (mardava). It does not bind the karma that is felt as pride, and it sheds what was bound before.

Source: Utt 29 s.70 · scan (checked letter by letter; matches the translation) · module. The word note glosses “conquering pride” as staying even-minded in honour and dishonour.

Humility (vinaya) as the daily antidote

  • Humility is the root, pride its enemy. Dharma grows from humility as a tree grows from its root, and liberation is its highest fruit (Dash 9.2.2). Pride destroys exactly this root (Dash 8.38). More on Vinaya.
  • Humility shows in what you do. The humble disciple carries out the teacher’s word, stays near him and reads his wishes (Utt 1.2). He takes even harsh correction as being for his good, while fools hear the same words with hatred (Utt 1.29). See Utt 1.
  • Lower seat, joined hands, “I will not do it again.” Keep your seat and walk lower than the guru’s; if you knock against him or his things, say “forgive me; I will not do it again” (Dash 9.2.17–18).
  • Utt 29 gives three practices that break pride. Serving the guru and fellow followers brings humility (s.6). Confessing your faults before others (garha) brings “the breaking of pride” (apuraskara) and turns you to wholesome activity (s.9). Bowing to the guru (vandana) destroys low-status karma (s.12). See Utt 29.
  • Honour does not make you great, and being ignored does not make you small. A monk should not be angry with one who does not bow to him, nor swell with pride when someone does (Dash 5.2.30). See Dash 5.

थंभा व कोहा व मयप्पमाया, गुरुस्सगासे विणयं ण सिक्खे ।
सो चेव उ तस्स अभूइभावो, फलं व कीयस्स वहाय होइ ॥

A monk who, from stiff pride, anger, conceit or laziness, does not practise humility in his guru’s presence brings about his own ruin by that very lack of humility, as the fruit of the bamboo brings about the bamboo’s death.

Source: Dash 9.1.1 · module (checked against the scan)

Do not praise yourself, do not look down on others

  • The two faces of pride. The commentary on Dash 8.30 says pride shows itself in two ways: praising oneself and showing contempt for others. Praising yourself is forgivable, it says; raising yourself by putting others down is not (Dash 8.30, commentary).
  • Do not call another “lax”. A true monk does not say of another, “this one is of bad conduct”, or say anything that would anger him. Knowing that each being’s merit and sin are its own, he does not raise himself up (Dash 10.18). The commentary’s cure: when ill feeling toward someone rises, remember that each soul acts out its own karma, and let others’ faults go (Dash 10.18, commentary). See Dash 10.
  • The one worthy of honour scorns no one. That means no child or elder, no man or woman, no monk or householder (Dash 9.3.12). Monks who keep different vows about cloth must not look down on each other (Ach 2.5.1.7–10); the commentary on Ach 1.6.3 says the same of monks with three robes, two, one or none, since each follows the Jina’s command according to his own strength. See Ach 1.6 and Ach 2.5.
  • When virtues grow, guard them most. The commentary says that as a rich man must guard against thieves, a soul growing in virtue must guard most of all against pride. The monk should think of himself as small, a servant, and remember the great souls before him (Dash 8.30, commentary).

ण बाहिरं परिभवे, अत्ताणं ण समुक्कसे ।
सुय लाभे ण मज्जेज्जा, जच्चा तवस्सि बुद्धिए ॥

He should not look down on anyone else, nor raise himself up. He should not grow proud on gaining learning, nor because of his birth, his austerity or his intelligence.

Source: Dash 8.30 · scan (checked letter by letter; the translation prints the verse number as [?], and the scan shows 30) · module

No one is high, no one is low: worth by deeds, not birth

  • Every soul has been both. This soul has been born many times in a high family and many times in a low one, so it is neither lower nor higher. Knowing this, who would boast of family or of pride (Ach 1.2.3 s.1)? The commentary quotes an old verse: there is no birth, womb, place or family where this soul has not been born and died countless times. See Ach 1.2.
  • The proud Brahmins and the outcaste monk. Brahmins proud of their caste mocked Harikeshbal, a monk born among outcastes (Utt 12.4–7). The answer: those who hold anger, pride, violence and lies are “empty of birth and learning” (Utt 12.14), and “the greatness of austerity is seen, no greatness of caste is seen” (Utt 12.37). The commentary adds that he was born an outcaste because of his pride in caste in a past life. See Utt 12.
  • By actions one is a brahmin. One becomes a brahmin, kshatriya, vaishya or shudra by one’s actions (Utt 25.33). A shaven head does not make a shramana, and “Om” does not make a brahmin; equanimity, celibacy, knowledge and austerity do (Utt 25.31–32). See Utt 25.
  • A monk by virtues, not by dress. One is a monk by virtues and not a monk by faults (Dash 9.3.11).

से असइं उच्चागोए, असइं णीयागोए । णो हीणे, णो अइरित्ते । णो पीहए । इति संखाए को गोयावाई ? को माणावाई ? कंसि वा एगे गिज्झे ? तम्हा पंडिए णो हरिसे, णो कुज्झे ।

This soul has been many times of high family and many times of low family. So it is neither lower nor higher; do not long for high status. Knowing this, who would boast of family? Who would boast of pride? On what would anyone fix his craving? So the wise one is neither elated nor upset.

Source: Ach 1.2.3 s.1 · scan p.123, p.124 (checked letter by letter; the translation’s असं, गोयांवाइ, एगो, कसि are corrected to the scan’s असइं, गोयावाई, एगे, कंसि) · module

Daily practices that cut the ego

  • Vandana: bowing destroys ego. The Avashyak commentary says bowing to the guru destroys ego, and that humility leads on to service, knowledge and restraint (Av 3, commentary, p. 96). The bow ends by asking pardon for any disrespect done “out of anger, pride, deceit or greed” (Av 3 s.1). The guru answers “I too ask forgiveness” (Av 3, p. 105). See Av 3.
  • Saying “be pleased with me”. The Tirthankaras are beyond being pleased, yet the devotee says it anyway, and in doing so gives up his ego (Av 2 v.5, commentary). See Av 2.
  • Censure and confession. In Samayik, nindami means censuring your fault inwardly. Garihami means confessing it before the guru, which the commentary calls harder “because it breaks one’s pride” (Av 1, p. 85). See Av 1.
  • Pratikraman names pride outright. It turns back from the four passions (conquer pride with gentleness) and from the eight grounds of pride (Av 4 L8). Of the 33 kinds of disrespect it lists, some are toward householders, men and women, and “all living beings” (Av 4 L8.45). See Av 4.
  • It ends in forgiveness. “I forgive all living beings; may all living beings forgive me” (Av 4 L10.1). Asking forgiveness brings gladness, friendship with all and fearlessness (Utt 29 s.19).

खामेमि सव्वे जीवा, सव्वे जीवा वि खमंतु मे ।
मित्तीमे सव्व भूएसू, वेरं मज्झं ण केणइ ॥१॥

I forgive all living beings; may all living beings forgive me. I have friendship with all beings and enmity with none.

Source: Av 4 L10.1 · module (checked against the scan)

Applying it

These are applications for a householder, not the books’ words.

  • Check the eight grounds. Caste, family, strength, looks, austerity, learning, gain and power cover most of what people boast about today: education, income, position, fasting, family name. When you feel yourself swelling, name which one it is (Utt 31.10; Av 4 L8).
  • Watch your pride in religion too. The texts name pride in austerity and learning, and even pride in being humble (Dash 8.30; Dash 9.4). A big fast or a lot of scripture learned is exactly where to be most careful.
  • Use the two-sided test. Do not raise yourself, and do not call others “lax” or lower them, even in your mind. When someone’s faults bother you, remember that their karma is theirs to answer for (Dash 8.30; Dash 10.18).
  • Treat caste and family as you would a hotel room. If every soul has been high and low many times, a family name is somewhere you are staying for a while, not a rank (Ach 1.2.3; Utt 25.33). Judge people, and yourself, by conduct.
  • Take correction as gain. When a parent, elder, colleague or teacher corrects you, think “this is for my good” before you defend yourself (Utt 1.29; Utt 29 s.70 gloss: stay even in honour and dishonour).
  • Make the daily rites do their work. Bow (vandana) with the meaning in mind, actually admit a fault to someone (garha), and close Pratikraman by asking forgiveness of the people you were proud with that day (Av 3; Av 4 L10.1; Utt 29 s.9).
